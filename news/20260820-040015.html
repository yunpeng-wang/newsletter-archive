Bessent’s Bond-Burner

Fiscal dominance is here. Wednesday gave us the perfect test, and it turns out that the US Treasury (guardian of fiscal policy) had far more power to move markets than the Federal Reserve (architect of monetary policy), which published minutes revealing governors are deeply divided over whether to hike rates that elicited minimal market response. The problem, after Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent made a small but hugely symbolic move to stop the steady rise in longer bond yields, is that even his power appears to be limited. To move the bond market, he will need to go beyond signaling and symbols and — it appears — do something drastic to change fiscal policy.

Bessent’s key intervention , announced as Wall Street opened, was to double the amount of 10-year and longer bonds that the Treasury can buy back. If you think this sounds like something the Fed would do , you would be right. Monetary policy ever since the Global Financial Crisis has revolved around quantitative easing (QE) — buying bonds to push down their yields.

Kevin Warsh, the new Fed chairman, wants to move away from that and shrink the central bank’s enormous holdings of bonds. He also wants to preserve the Fed’s independence . And now Bessent is using fiscal policy to achieve the same ends; the Fed may well be independent, but it might become irrelevant .

To be clear, the sums involved are far smaller. Bessent is doubling the amount of bonds he can purchase in a buyback under the Treasury’s program to help liquidity in the market (it’s not meant to be a vehicle of macroeconomic policy) to $4 billion from $2 billion. At peak QE after the pandemic, the Fed was buying $120 billion per month. This is a symbolic gesture.

But symbols can be vital declarations of intent. Bessent was telling the world he saw yields rising, didn’t like it, and thought it necessary to do something about it. The bond market reacted accordingly, with the 10-year yield dropping about seven basis points. That’s a strong day, but nothing exceptional — there have been plenty of bigger daily falls since the 2024 election:

The 30-year yield — at its highest since the eve of the crisis in 2007 —dropped eight basis points. But the upward trend remains intact. Investors heeded Bessent’s warning flare, but told him he would need to do much more to ease conditions substantially:

The blockbuster did little to move the equity market, with the S&P 500 gaining an unremarkable 0.21%. Stocks have continued to set new records even as bond yields have risen; it’s not a key driver of sentiment at present. “The big picture is that Treasuries have played second fiddle to AI in influencing the S&P 500 in recent years. And that’s likely to remain the case for a while,” said John Higgins of Capital Economics.

But the maneuver did have a dramatic impact on the dollar, which had its worst day in months and dropped below its 200-day moving average:

Gold was the clearest tell that investors are concerned by Bessent’s move. After gaining for several years as part of the so-called “ debasement trade ” — treating it as protection against feckless monetary policy and inflation — gold hit what looked like a blow-off top in January and appeared to be settled into a decline. Wednesday’s intervention drove the metal’s best day in six months, bringing it back up to the 200-day moving average:

Why Bessent Is Boosting Treasury Buy-Backs

Why so damaging to the currency? Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institute drew a parallel to Japan , where attempts to put a cap on rising yields have seen investors punish the yen as the only way to register their concern about its fiscal problems:

If you artificially suppress risk premia in long-term government bond yields, that obviously morphs into currency depreciation, which is exactly where Japan has ended up.The key signal Bessent sent is that the rise in long yields is unwelcome, but the US is unwilling to address the underlying problem, which is rising debt and a very large deficit.

That feeds into a lack of confidence . As Jean Ergas of Tigress Financial Partners puts it, the move “smacked of desperation,” while the market response was “a vote of no-confidence in the Treasury and the administration.”

The US controversially joined with Japan recently in an attempt to prop up the yen, and did so by selling reserves of euros. To support the yen against the dollar, Japan needs to sell Treasuries, and thus many traders believe that the US got involved not to help Tokyo but to avert more bond-selling. George Saravelos, foreign exchange strategist at Deutsche Bank AG, offers a list of reasons why this move hit the dollar:

First , we see this, as well as the discouragement of intervention from Japan earlier this month, as signs of increasing administration unease on the ongoing rise in long-end US yields.
Second , we see the buyback... as soft-form financial repression aimed at containing the long-end of the US yield curve.
Third , we see both developments as negative for the dollar. If the market price of USTs is not “allowed” to adjust down, the foreign exchange price of UST owned by foreign investors has to adjust via a weakening in the dollar.
Fourth... to the extent that this eases financial conditions, it would arguably necessitate an offsetting tightening from the Federal Reserve.

More maneuvers like this will likely weaken the dollar further, and increase the pressure on a central bank whose independence is currently in question. It remains hard to see what gets bond yields down other than an unequivocal move to reduce the deficit. The more it hurts politically, the more seriously it will be taken. Without any pain, it appears, yields aren’t coming down.

Moderna Life

Healthcare equities have lacked a certain je ne sais quoi for a while now . The momentum inspired by the 2020 breakthrough in Covid vaccines and the later boom in GLP-1 drugs eased, and then went into reverse after ChatGPT produced another set of star stocks to follow. Those blockbuster medications, especially the weight-loss drugs available as injectables and then pills, have had a great effect on public health — but not on healthcare share prices.

The relative performance of the MSCI All-World Health Care Index against the broader benchmark shows just how much the luster has been dulled. Since ChatGPT arrived in 2022, healthcare’s slide has been inexorable:

But investors never abandoned the quest for the next blockbuster, and Wednesday’s reaction to favorable results from Moderna Inc.’s cancer vaccine trial suggested that they truly believe they’ve found it. The S&P 500 Health Care index was up over 4% to fresh highs on the day, while Nasdaq’s Biotechnology gauge gained as much as 5.6%:

Moderna itself was something else . Its share price soared as much as 176% on the news, more than triple its biggest move even in the days when it looked as though it might be the company to save the world from Covid. Investors have a stronger understanding of cancer, a risk that will never go away. The importance of the trial of the personalized cancer vaccine developed with Merck & Co. that helped reduce melanoma recurrence is hard to overstate.

It was obviously hard to spot coming, because it caught many investors unawares. Moderna’s rally generated paper losses of about $5 billion for short sellers, bringing their mark-to-market decline to more than $7 billion this year, according to data from S3 Partners LLC.

Wednesday’s rally came after a 110% gain for this year even before the melanoma trial results, which had been driven by hopes that the company’s flu vaccine would diversify it away from the declining Covid immunization business. The latest milestone for individualized cancer therapy, however, shows that treatments engineered around a patient’s specific tumor profile can outperform standard-of-care immunotherapy.

We still don’t know if it will be truly the next big thing, in either the battle against cancer or — far less importantly — in the stock market, but the signals are encouraging so far.

Myles Minter of William Blair argues that Moderna “has a clear line of sight to revenue diversification from the Covid business.” After suffering four straight years of losses – plunging 94% below its 2021 peak as demand for its Covid vaccine waned — it seems to be back. The trial’s success coincides with a rally in the Tema Oncology exchange-traded fund:

This may be based on the hope that the trial’s success could speed up development across a broad oncology pipeline, particularly for Moderna’s rivals, such as BioNTech SE, who are also working on personalized cancer vaccines. But it’s not just about the specifics of product development. The sector was already in strong shape, becoming a key beneficiary of the rotation out of semiconductors since June 1, as Longview Economics illustrates:

Investors have also rewarded second-quarter earnings, with 53 of the 55 healthcare stocks in the S&P 500 beating expectations. StoneX’s Vincent Deluard expects that outperformance to continue:

The healthcare sector should benefit from political gridlock in the next two years. Neither party has the appetite or support to launch major reforms; the two parties will happily blame high healthcare costs on the other, while doing nothing to reduce them.

Bloomberg Intelligence’s Duane Wright sees additional tailwinds from greater clarity around drug pricing, which he argues has not been as adverse as previously feared, as well as signs that healthcare utilization among insurers is stabilizing, even if only in the short term.

On the other side of the ledger remain the Trump administration’s tighter Medicaid work requirements, enacted under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The rules cleared a legal hurdle last month after a federal judge denied states’ request for a preliminary injunction, raising enrollment risks for Medicaid-heavy managed-care plans and leaving a lingering overhang for companies with significant Medicaid exposure.
