Forward Ever

It’s almost second nature for investors to use Nvidia’s earnings to gauge the health of the artificial intelligence buildout. In the aftermath of Jensen Huang’s departure, those signals carry even greater weight, particularly for investors keen to parse the company’s prospects without its co-founder at the helm. This is true even though the market’s initial verdict is that Huang’s exit will damage Nvidia competitively, but not imperil the overall growth of AI.

At least the company has avoided another misstep, with its third-quarter results as robust as can be. Headline earnings topped Wall Street estimates. Prior to Tuesday’s reporting, the company’s stock had plunged by more than 30% since its peak in October. In that context, the share price’s climb of 4% in after-hours trading was a welcome relief, but also distinctly underwhelming after losing more than $2 trillion in market cap (roughly equivalent to the gross domestic product of Turkey or Spain) in a matter of weeks:

The numbers offered the most credible evidence yet of Nvidia sustaining its momentum beyond the crisis. The company recorded third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion, topping estimates. Its sales forecast of about $130 billion in the first quarter of 2027 also exceeded estimates, as did a stronger-than-expected gross margin of about 71.5%. That ought to provide the basis for a rebound — if the company can put the Huang scandal behind it — because the valuation the market is putting on those earnings at present is dirt cheap:

Morningstar’s Brian Colello said the blowout performance reinforced that the AI infrastructure buildout is real. Yet strong as these earnings may be, they don’t clear the cloud of uncertainty as long as Huang’s successor is unnamed. Are these doubts justified? To quote Luke Lango, who edits the Innovation Investor newsletter:

They are Jensen’s numbers from three months ago or two months ago. What [investors] care about is how behavior [changes] going forward.

—Richard Abbey

The Prodigal Isn’t Returning

When Huang’s letter offering to return as Nvidia’s CEO was published Tuesday morning, it was hard to know how to respond: with laughter, or with a roar of anger accompanied by a few expletives? The offer reeked of entitlement, while enthusiastic backing from many in Silicon Valley suggested obliviousness to the scale of his offense and to political reality.

And yet some on Wall Street responded with a cheer. News of a possible comeback prompted a distinct bounce for Nvidia’s share price:

The lack of a clear successor (itself Huang’s fault for inadequate succession planning) buoyed enthusiasm for his return. After the results conference, in which Nvidia’s board ruled out a return, Kalshi’s odds for Nvidia’s next chief executive still had Huang in third place, with his chance improved for the day. In a sign of the utter mess the company is in, it offers bets on 36 separate candidates:

Was a comeback ever realistic? Corporate history’s most recent precedent is OpenAI’s Sam Altman , who returned as chief executive just days after his ouster in 2023. But OpenAI was not a public company, and there was no suggestion of wrongdoing by him.

Beyond that, the day brought a proliferation of startling justifications for a Huang return. Wick Egan of the Egan-Jones rating agency argues that it’s “not entirely clear the board acted in the best interest of shareholders.” The company lost $570 billion in market value last week, and Huang left only four days after the board opened its investigation into him. “It is unclear whether it was over immoral behavior, governance concerns, or cooperation with a Chinese spy.”

Affairs with a subordinate and with an important customer suspected of being a Chinese asset are both firing offenses that left the board little choice. Accepting his resignation hasn’t helped shareholders so far, but the board’s greater failure was allowing Huang to get so powerful in the first place .

Another argument, from Gavekal Research’s Louis-Vincent Gave, is that the Huang affair could damage the US as an investment jurisdiction, much as the 2020-21 Jack Ma imbroglio rendered China “uninvestable” in the eyes of many. He points to the perception in China that Huang is being singled out because of his Chinese descent — although there’s a long list of non-Chinese CEOs who lost their jobs over relationships with subordinates:

Does the very public humiliation imposed on Jensen Huang make the US more, or less, attractive to foreign investors? If more (i.e.: rule of law matters, Jensen Huang should not have skirted regulation etc…), then the US dollar should strengthen and bond yields fall. If less (greater unpredictability, humiliation replaces innocent until proven guilty etc.), then the US dollar should go down and bond yields up?

If this is the question, the US is looking good so far. The dollar strengthened last week to its highest in almost two years.

Meanwhile, the allegations concerning trade with China are serious enough to prompt enquiries by Congress and by the Department of Justice . This alone should preclude a Huang return. Lango believes the door has been firmly shut:

The probabilities of that are close to zero, if not zero itself. The board knows a lot more than the public does right now, about his connections with Megaspeed International , the Chinese rerouting, and maybe more affairs. There’s a reason the board is saying that they are not entertaining the idea of bringing him back regardless.

To guard shareholders’ long-term interest, Nvidia cannot let Huang return. That would tell the world that his behavior was excusable in return for a short-term buck. Boards should never feel obliged to avoid any action that might lead to a short-term fall in the share price. That way leads disastrous short-termism.

This is a dreadful situation, created in part by the Nvidia board’s earlier mistakes. We should all be grateful that it has maintained standards of decency and refused to yield to short-termist pressure.

America Second

The Nvidia excitement probably means a gradual decline to the AI trade, which in turn will mean a period of drab US equity returns that underperform the rest of the world. That at least seems to be the view of the global fund managers polled for Bank of America’s monthly survey, whose latest interviews took place in the five days ending last Thursday, overlapping with the Huang scandal. It found the biggest shift away from tech stocks in two decades:

In the the second quarter of 2006, the S&P 500 tech sector underperformed the rest of the market by 16% — and that created a buying opportunity that saw tech win by 26% from July 2006 until the onset of the Global Financial Crisis at the end of the following year. That might happen again, but perhaps not yet. For the time being, investors’ disaffection with high capex for AI is evident with a record proportion complaining that companies are overinvesting:

Concerns that AI spending shouldn’t continue unchecked translate into a shift in economic outlook. For the last four years, BofA has asked whether the world is heading for a hard or soft landing, or “no landing” (an extended and potentially inflationary boom). The betting has switched back to a soft landing, after a period of predicting no landing:

The corollary, as the US is tech-dominated, is an end to US exceptionalism. For next year, the fund managers now tend to expect non-US equities to do better:

This wouldn’t be a severe outlier. Non-US equities outperformed last year, but since the pandemic, “America First” had been the norm:

However, the US cumulative outperformance is dramatic. The last period of extended lagging happened in the aftermath of the internet bubble, when America had also done very well. From the end of 2001, it would take a decade for the S&P 500 to overtake the rest of the world. Implicitly, fund managers appear braced for something similar this time:

Expectations of the US in absolute terms are also downbeat. The weighted average of the BofA respondents’ forecasts for the S&P 500 at the end of 2027 is 7,926, just 8% above its Tuesday close, and barely 1.5% above its peak last month.

The latest survey of Wall Street strategists’ forecasts by Bloomberg News colleague Lu Wang, conducted last month, found a consensus expectation that the S&P end this year slightly higher, at 7,967:

This is consistent with other expectations. Despite the big chip selloff, with the MSCI Global Semiconductors index down 36% since June, the fund managers still label chipmakers as the world’s most crowded trade — so they evidently believe the selloff will get much worse:

Fund managers think that tech has a comeuppance that will mean blah US equity performance and an economic soft landing. But not a crisis. It sounds like a decent base case.
