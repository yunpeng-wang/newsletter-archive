Anthropic

There are two basic ways to think about what stocks to own. One is that you make a list of the stocks you want to buy, and you buy them. If you have a good reason to buy a stock, you buy it; if not, not. The default is not. If you have no opinions about a stock, you don’t buy it.

The other is that you make a list of the stocks in the world, and for each stock on the list, you ask yourself: “Do I have a reason to overweight or underweight this stock?” The default is owning the market portfolio: If you have no opinions about a stock, or ambivalent or confused opinions, you tend to just own the average amount of the stock, its market capitalization weighting in the market portfolio. If you have reason to think that the stock is better than the market thinks it is, buy more of it. If you have reason to think that it’s worse than the market thinks it is, buy less of it, or zero, or go short. But you start from the premise that the market has a pretty good idea of what stocks to own, and that if you have no special information, then you should just do what the market tells you.

I think it is fair to say that the second approach — defaulting to the market weight and overweighting or underweighting when you have reason to — was virtually unheard-of 100 years ago and is quite standard now. Many, many investors mostly buy index funds: They assume that the market knows better than they do, so they buy the stocks that the market likes. But many active investors use the same intellectual framework: They run large diversified portfolios and pick stocks to overweight and underweight against the background assumption that the default is the market weight. [1]

The two approaches suggest different ideas of risk. In the first approach, each stock is risky, and you should only make a risky bet on a stock if you have a good reason to. In the second approach, investors expect to earn the market return on average, and there is symmetrical risk around that return. If the market returns 8% and you earn 2%, that’s bad; in the relevant sense you have lost 6%. Owning too little of a stock is as risky as owning too much of it, and you need a good reason to take either risk.

In practice, this often comes up in debates about index inclusion. We talked about it a lot when SpaceX went public and various index providers contemplated changing their rules to include SpaceX in their indexes. If SpaceX was in an index, then (1) index funds indexed to that index would have to buy it in proportion to its index weight and (2) active investors benchmarked to that index would have to think about it in proportion to its index weight. They would have to make a decision to overweight or underweight or market-weight SpaceX, and because SpaceX is so big (and such a big percentage of the index), that decision had a lot of risk. If they hated SpaceX, they didn’t have to buy it, but if it then outperformed the market, they would underperform. (And vice versa.) Index inclusion would not force any active investors to buy SpaceX stock, but it would force them to develop an informed opinion about SpaceX stock. Or not, and just default to buying it in proportion to its market weight.

But the index-inclusion stuff is in some sense fake, just a minor artifact of market structure. The real question is not “what does the S&P 500 Index committee think about this stock” but rather “what does the market think about this stock?” If the market is telling you that, you know, 10% of the world’s future economic growth will come from one company, then you have to decide whether or not to buy that company’s stock at the market price, and your decision has high stakes. If it’s not in your benchmark index, that gives you an excuse to ignore it, but not a very good one. If in fact that company is responsible for 12% of future economic growth, you will miss out on a lot by ignoring it.

The main theme of US financial markets over the last four years has been artificial intelligence. The market is telling you that a huge portion of future economic growth will come from AI . It is telling you that in the form of the stock prices of Alphabet and Meta and SpaceX and Nvidia, and in the form of the hundreds of billions of dollars of debt being raised to build data centers, and in the form of the huge private valuations of early-stage AI companies, and in many other ways.

Anthropic PBC went public yesterday, upsizing its initial public offering to about $107 billion and pricing it at $83 per share, for a fully diluted market capitalization of about $1.5 trillion. That’s the biggest IPO ever, but it’s not that big. Anthropic is on the order of 2% of the total size of the US public stock market. There’s a whole other 98% out there. But Anthropic is, like, conceptually bigger than that. The market is largely the AI trade, and the big frontier AI labs are arguably at the center of the AI trade, and Anthropic is the first pure-play frontier AI lab to go public. If you are an investor, you have to have some mental model of the AI trade, because the AI trade is virtually all there is. If you have a mental model of the AI trade, you probably have some thesis about how the frontier labs fit into it, whether they will capture more or less of the profits from the AI trade than the market expects. And if you have that thesis about the frontier labs, you probably have a view about Anthropic’s valuation.

And all of this is extremely uncertain and science-fictional, and you might not feel confident about your view, and you might throw your hands up and say “Anthropic could be worth $20 trillion or $0 in three years, how do I know?” That’s a reasonable thing to think; it’s roughly my view, though I am not paid to make investing decisions. But if you think that, and you are a professional (or amateur) investor, the default response might be to … buy stock in the IPO? Anthropic seems important, the market is telling you it is important, you have no idea, so who are you to second-guess the market? “Anthropic is a ‘must own,’” Craig Coben writes at the Financial Times :

Many investors I spoke to had reservations about the nosebleed valuation for Anthropic’s IPO, about the huge capex required and about the threat of commoditisation. But, they kept saying, the stock is a must-own. You have to buy it. …

Every global fund needs exposure to AI. Anthropic is the first major publicly listed pure-play frontier AI model developer. Google and Amazon offer indirect exposure due to their shareholding in Anthropic and cloud businesses, but that’s not quite the same thing. You don’t want to be underweight — or worse, unexposed — to a company that could turn into one of the defining businesses of our era.

The risks are symmetrical. You might as well own some.

That’s not investing advice! (“It’s all right to pass on Anthropic PBC’s initial public offering,” writes my Bloomberg Opinion colleague Nir Kaissar .) It is more, like, a commentary on IPO tactics. Bloomberg reports :

Anthropic PBC’s initial public offering left more than half of the institutional investors bidding for stock without any shares, according to people familiar with the matter.

Allocations of the shares were heavily skewed toward US-based investors, with a large proportion of orders coming in from outside the country getting no allocation, they said. About 1,200 institutional investors placed orders, some of the people said.

We talked yesterday about the fact that investors in hot IPOs tend to inflate their orders. If some big investor put in an order for 20 million shares, that might mean that it wanted 5 million but anticipated that its order would be cut back because there was so much demand. Therefore, some of the demand for the stock — about $500 billion of demand for about $100 billion of stock — was fake, and if Anthropic had actually tried to sell $500 billion of stock it probably couldn’t have. [2] But obviously a lot of the demand was real, and a lot of the unmet demand was real. If a big investor put in an order for 1 million shares, it probably wanted a lot of them, and if it got zero shares, it probably still wants them. “It’s too risky not to own this AI stock,” it was probably thinking this morning. [3]

And so it put in orders to buy shares when Anthropic opened for trading. The stock opened at 11:48 this morning, with about 108 million shares trading in the opening auction at $111.50 , about a 34% pop from the $83 IPO price. A lot of investors decided they needed to own some Anthropic, put in orders to buy it in the IPO, didn’t get any, and came back to the market today to buy what they needed.

That’s how it’s supposed to work; that’s how the banks manage the IPO to make sure it “trades well” (i.e., up) when it opens. Some buyers in the IPO want to hold the stock forever, but some were sellers this morning, and they needed buyers. Anthropic’s banks didn’t just line up buyers for the IPO; they also lined up buyers for the post-IPO trading today, by zeroing a lot of the bidders in the IPO.

Anyway, the stock traded down after that opening cross; at about 2 p.m. it was trading around $95.79 per share, or about 15% above the IPO price. The traditional target IPO pop is about 20% , so they got pretty close.

Prepaid energy

Here’s a trade:

  1. You are the mayor of a town somewhere in the US. Your town has some expenses. Say your municipal gas utility needs to buy natural gas every year to deliver to residential customers in your town. You expect to spend about $200 on gas every year.
  2. You can borrow money by issuing municipal bonds to investors. The investors don’t pay federal income tax on the interest they receive on these bonds, so municipal bonds tend to have low interest rates. Say you can issue a 10-year bond that pays 3.5% per year .
  3. You can buy bonds issued by Alphabet Inc. Those are rated AA and pretty safe, and they pay about 5.5% interest for 10 years. Alphabet pays a higher interest rate than you do because the interest on its bonds is taxable and the interest on your bonds isn’t. But you don’t pay federal income tax on the interest you receive on those bonds, because you are a municipality and don’t pay income tax at all .
  4. You sell $10,000 of municipal bonds and invest the $10,000 of proceeds in Alphabet bonds. You pay $350 per year (3.5%) of interest on the bonds you sold, and receive $550 per year (5.5%) of interest on the bonds you bought, for a net profit of $200 per year.
  5. You spend the $200 per year buying gas for your customers.

Nothing here is tax advice, but, great trade. Here’s a tweak to make it even better. You call up your gas supplier and say “look, I need $200 worth of gas a year for 10 years. I’d like to enter into a long-term contract to get all that gas at a fixed price. Could you do that?” The gas supplier says “eh, I don’t know, you are just some local town utility; am I sure you’ll be able to pay me in 10 years?” You say “oh, no, it’s all money-good; I’ve got some Alphabet bonds set aside to pay my gas costs. If you want, I could put them in escrow with you, so you know you’ll get your $200 a year directly from Alphabet.” Your gas supplier says “oh that’s a great deal, sure, if I can get my payments from Alphabet I’ll lock in a price and even give you a discount; I will charge you $180 for $200 worth of gas.”

On the one hand, sure, good trade. On the other hand, the essential thing that is going on here is a tax arbitrage. You are issuing bonds at 3.5% because they are tax-exempt, using the money to buy other bonds at 5.5% because they are taxable, and clipping the 2% spread. Your bond buyers don’t pay taxes on the interest you pay, you don’t pay taxes on the interest you receive, and you are making that 2% profit strictly at the expense of the US Internal Revenue Service. The stuff about gas is irrelevant. You could spend the $200 on increasing your own salary or speculating on the stock market. That $200 is just free money.

This is too good a trick. The IRS is aware of this, and this trade does not actually work. Municipal bond interest is not tax-free if the bond is an “arbitrage bond,” meaning that “any portion of the proceeds” of the bond are used “to acquire higher yielding investments.” [4] Towns can’t just go issue tax-free bonds and use the proceeds to buy Alphabet bonds. That would be stupid.

Nonetheless! Bloomberg’s Erin Hudson and Amanda Albright report :

Google parent Alphabet Inc. is planning to participate in two upcoming prepaid energy transactions totaling $3 billion.

Alphabet is listed as the funding recipient on two bond offerings that will be issued by the Southeast Energy Authority, according to preliminary bond documents posted Monday. Both transactions are being managed by Goldman Sachs Group Inc., one of the leading underwriters of prepaid deals.

The deals come about two weeks after Alphabet’s last transaction. It was the funding recipient on a roughly $1.6 billion deal that priced Nov. 4.

Prepaid energy bonds are a fast-growing sector in the tax-exempt debt market for local and state governments. The sector has already set a new record for annual issuance with about $40 billion in prepaid energy bonds sold so far this year, compared to the prior record of $31 billion last year, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. …

Prepaid deals are complex transactions that allow utilities to secure a discounted supply of electricity or gas. They involve a financial middleman, known as a funding recipient, which receives the proceeds of the bond sale upfront in exchange for making payments over time that facilitate the delivery of the energy to the participating utility.

The utility in both upcoming SEA transactions is the Salt River Project Agricultural Improvement and Power District, which provides electricity to customers in Arizona’s Maricopa, Gila and Pinal counties.

Part of the prepaid sector’s growth is attributed is the growing number of companies that are willing to participate in the transactions as funding recipients. Historically, the role was played by banks, but now firms including insurance companies, a real estate investment trust and most recently, Alphabet have stepped into the market as funding recipients.

A “prepaid energy bond” is, in essence, the trade I started with. [5] Some municipal utility issues tax-exempt bonds and gives the proceeds to a bank or, increasingly, a tech company. The funding recipient (here, Alphabet) gets the money, which it can use for its purposes. In exchange, it promises to pay some gas or electricity supplier on behalf of the municipal utility, and the supplier agrees to deliver gas or electricity to the utility. In general , arbitrage bonds are not allowed, but there are specific rules allowing for this particular trade. A municipality can’t issue tax-exempt bonds to buy Alphabet bonds and use the spread to pay the mayor’s salary or speculate on stocks, but it can issue tax-exempt bonds to invest with Alphabet to pay for gas.

Why is Alphabet in this business? Part of the answer is that money is money, these deals provide funding for Alphabet, and it presumably gets a cut of the tax savings in the form of cheaper financing. But part of the answer is that, 10 years ago, no one would have said that Google was in the electricity and natural gas business, but now it absolutely is. Here’s a BlackRock research note on prepaid gas bonds from last month:

Prepaid gas issuance has grown rapidly, surpassing $100 billion and increasing by more than 25% annually since 2020. This reflects the relative appeal of tax-exempt financing in a higher rate environment. At the same time, rising power demand from AI hyperscalers could drive more long-term gas supply agreements, creating opportunities for these companies to access the municipal market and potentially accelerate prepaid gas issuance.

Alphabet is now in the long-term energy supply business, and it might as well do a little tax arbitrage while it’s there.

Anthropic: Lockups

The Anthropic IPO is, unsurprisingly, going to make its employees rich and liquid. The Wall Street Journal notes :

A majority of the company’s more than 5,000 employees are likely to become millionaires, according to estimates by Hill.com, a marketplace for private-company shares. That includes roughly 2,800 employees whose stakes are likely to be worth at least $10 million, and 600 who are likely to be sitting on holdings exceeding $100 million.

I have written a few times around here about the central problem of AI, which is that the going rate for a good AI researcher is “enough to retire immediately.” If your top 600 employees all get paid $100 million at the same time, you cannot really expect them all to stick around to work long hours building AI. I have mentioned various solutions to this problem, including “ sudden wealth syndrome ” diagnoses, “$30 million will buy you a two-bedroom in the San Francisco metro area,” and AI researchers’ sheer love of the game, which keeps them motivated despite their economic ability to buy an island and stop working. Two other solutions are:

  1. Recursive self-improvement, where Anthropic replaces AI researchers with the AI that they built, and the AI itself builds the next AI model; and
  2. In Anthropic’s case, lockups, so that employees can’t immediately sell their $100 million of stock, and have to keep working for a while to make sure that it keeps its value.

Those two can work together, I suppose: Anthropic has until the lockups expire to build AI tools that can replace its researchers.

Situational Awareness 2.0

An approximate description of Situational Awareness LP, Leopold Aschenbrenner’s artificial-intelligence-focused hedge fund, is:

  1. It was a big AI hedge fund with billions of dollars of assets, owning both bets on public companies and shares of private AI companies, including a big slug of Anthropic.
  2. Over time, it borrowed more money to make more public bets.
  3. These bets went very well until they didn’t , at which point Situational Awareness got margin calls and had to sell basically all of its public positions to pay them.
  4. It retreated, chastened, to investing mostly without leverage and mostly in private companies.
  5. Now Anthropic is going public.
  6. We’re back, baby!

The Financial Times reports :

Leopold Aschenbrenner is plotting an audacious attempt to revive Situational Awareness just four months after the hedge fund was nearly toppled by bad bets on AI stocks.

The 25-year-old has in recent weeks discussed plans for a new iteration of the fund, including a return to leveraged trading and raising new capital, according to people briefed on the matter. The effort has been dubbed “Situational Awareness 2.0” in conversations with prime brokers and investors.

Aschenbrenner’s push to reboot Situational Awareness will hinge on his ability to convince investors and Wall Street banks that he can manage risk so soon after the AI-focused vehicle went from overseeing more than $20bn to needing a rescue from Ken Griffin’s Citadel in a matter of days this summer. The fund still manages more than $10bn, mostly in private investments.

“It’s bad form to blow up repeatedly,” said one investor. “So [Aschenbrenner] really wants to avoid being in that situation again, but I do think he expects to be [managing a public book] in a significant way.”

Obviously part of this is normal hedge-fund stuff. Aschenbrenner’s thesis has been largely correct with some large blips. Now he is sadder and wiser, a wounded lion . It is good for your financial career to lose billions of dollars, and now Aschenbrenner has that advantage.

But part of it is just that, when you run a $10 billion portfolio of stock in private tech companies, there is only so much you can do . You can’t trade that stock easily, or speculate on options, or run a long/short book, or do complicated relative-value trades or short-term bets on earnings. If you go to a bank and say “I have $5 billion of private Anthropic stock, can you lend me 80% of its value so I can buy more,” the bank will say no. If you go to a bank and say “I have $5 billion of private Anthropic stock, can you lend me 50% of its value so I can buy more,” the bank will say “sure, absolutely, just fill out this form,” but when you write “Situational Awareness” at the top of the form they will say “oh wait you’re that guy” and turn you down.

But now Aschenbrenner is just automatically back to managing a public book in a significant way: $5 billion of his private book became public. Once you have $5 billion of public Anthropic stock, that is going to be burning a bit of a hole in your pocket. Even if you’re that guy, someone will give you a nice margin loan against a 0.3% stake in a giant liquid public company. You can buy more if you think it will go up, or sell some if you think it will go down, or whatever. The world opens back up to you. Situational Awareness’s shares might be subject to a post-IPO lockup, which would delay the full range of possibilities, but even locked-up public stock is of more interest to a prime broker than private stock. Anthropic’s IPO is a natural way for him to get back in the game.

Anthropic is quite cash rich for a company doing an IPO, with about $132 billion of cash and short-term investments even before the IPO proceeds, but it is also extraordinarily cash hungry for a software company, with about $900 billion of commitments to pay for compute. Why not take as much money as possible now? There are several good reasons, including that those orders are not fully binding, and if the banks had called everyone to say “we upsized to $500 billion and you’re filled 100%,” a lot of investors would have said “no we aren’t.” Also Anthropic doesn't actually want that much dilution, and expects to pay for that compute out of its revenue. But a simpler and more mechanical reason is that US Securities and Exchange Commission rules don’t really allow companies to raise the size or price of an IPO by more than 20% without revising the prospectus. Anthropic originally filed to sell 825 million shares and ended up selling 980 million , about a 19% increase.
