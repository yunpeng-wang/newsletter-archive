Maybe 6.5% Is the New 5%. Or 7%

When does middle age start? If you’d asked me in my teens, I would have said my 30th birthday. When that date arrived, I revised my opinion with the benefit of extra years and said 50. Now, having just passed 60, I will reluctantly concede that middle age is here. It arrived a few months ago.

I don’t think I’m alone in my attitude to aging, a great and unavoidable fact of our humanity which we we all have to deal with, or not deal with, in our own way. As a rather cruel parody of The Who’s My Generation has it:

People try to put is down
Just because we’re still around.

No wonder Pete Townshend, now in his 80s, hates playing the song . And it seems the same psychology is at work among asset allocators as they deal with another fact of life that seems just as inevitable — the inexorable rise in longer US Treasury yields.

For months, we have lived with the widespread and unquantified belief that 5.0% on the 10-year Treasury yield would be the landmark, or tipping point , when the stock market had no choice but to take notice. That, after all, is what happened when the 10-year crashed through 5% to trigger the financial crisis in 2007 . Now, it’s been and gone, like a 30th birthday, and equity investors are still partying like a group of twentysomethings.

At what point will they have to take rising 10-year yields seriously? The latest Bank of America survey of global fund managers asked the question, and found that 64% think it won’t come until 6%, with many thinking it will need to go well beyond that:

If we can wait that long, that would help explain the calm emanating from stock markets. Even more importantly, a majority of fund managers are confident that 6% yields will never happen (at least not in this cycle). A number of redoubtable bond market bears have said in recent weeks that they’re shifting toward positivity, with Pimco’s Daniel Ivascyn trimming a long-standing underweight in Treasuries , while BlackRock’s Jean Boivin and Rick Rieder have turned bullish on shorter-term bonds . The broader mass of fund managers seems to agree with them. A slim majority once more expects longer-term rates to reduce from here, not rise, a state of affairs seldom seen in this century:

Abetting this is a growing sense that the angst about hawkishness at the Federal Reserve, which set in in a big way after Chairman Kevin Warsh’s speech at Jackson Hole in August, has been badly overdone. When the New York Fed President John Williams told Bloomberg on Wednesday that there needn’t be any rate hikes next year , he confirmed a gentle easing in expectations embodied in the futures market. Further calming would bring that much more support to equities, confirming that surviving the date with destiny at 5% was all the mattered:

The other big impulse continues to be tech sector earnings which, with all due deference to the big banks who have just reported, now dwarf all others in importance. Ajay Rajadhyaksha of Barclays lays out how important semiconductors alone have become, accounting for almost a fifth of the S&P 500’s market cap, and more than a third of aggregate earnings growth:

Thankfully, just as fund managers are braced for bond yields to start declining, they’re also positioned for this incredible growth to continue. Earnings expectations for the SOX semiconductor index, as measured by Bloomberg, have done nothing but rise:

Arguably, the key will be capital expenditures. A few companies are spending enormous sums of money and have separated themselves from all others, as shown in this remarkable chart, drawn with the aid of Bloomberg’s AskB AI tool:

If this extraordinary phenomenon continues, then fund managers’ optimism will have been vindicated. But it’s asking a lot. Indeed, putting off the moment when share prices at last react to the tectonic shift in bonds looks barely any less far-fetched than an attempt to postpone the onset of middle age permanently.

Banking Blues

Wall Street banks were hoping to redeem themselves after underperforming the broader market. But they were disappointed as the relatively strong earnings they announced on Tuesday failed to boost their share prices. Ahead of this week, the group was down 6% since the second quarter, even as the broader markets rose 4.6%. Apart from JPMorgan, which reported earnings below estimates, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, and Wells Fargo all either met or beat estimates. It made little difference for investors:

Why so bad? We still await a full set of results from all six major banks — Bank of America and Morgan Stanley are set to report within hours of your receiving this — for the clearest take from investors, but the KBW Bank Index’s lackluster performance is already hard to ignore:

It wasn’t as though these results were without their bright spots. JPMorgan, Citigroup and Wells Fargo reported a combined $56.5 billion in net interest income for the third quarter, a fifth straight quarterly record for the profits earned from lending, banks’ traditional engine:

The Fed’s return to tighter monetary policy should boost net interest income further unless it goes so far as to dissuade customers from borrowing. JPMorgan is guiding to expect higher interest income next year, which suggests confidence in continuing customer appetite. The other risk is that the Fed forces the yield curve into inversion (so longer bonds yield less than short-term ones), which is a classic sign of recession but also makes it far harder for banks to make money from lending.

A look at banks’ relative performance on the stock market suggests that a flattening yield curve over the last few months as bonds have sold off has soured investors:

The greater problems came in trading, an increasingly important business for the traditional leading banks. They suffered a broad-based shortfall in fixed-income, currencies and commodities revenue compared to estimates. Of the four that reported, only Citi topped analysts’ estimates, while JPMorgan’s fell 4% year-on-year, the first decline in over two years.

Bloomberg Intelligence’s Herman Chan says that falling fixed-income trading revenue might reflect the higher-for-longer rate outlook.

For now, the takeaway from the fitful re-steepening of the yield curve amid the bond market ructions is that stock markets no longer necessarily buy into the assumption that higher rates will benefit banks. Rather, they appear to be weighing potential higher rates against the risks they pose to bank balance sheets. Perhaps this helps explain why optimistic 2007 net interest income forecasts haven’t helped banks’ share prices much.

Meanwhile, equities trading desks brought in a record haul, making up for the disappointment. The combination of market volatility and AI-driven equity issuance continues to create a great environment for them to generate revenues. Goldman’s recorded a 72% revenue increase over the previous quarter, leading peers and topping estimates. JPMorgan’s revenue rose almost 50%, with notable outperformance in prime and derivatives. Citigroup came in with a 14% increase, falling below analyst estimates.

They’re not the only ones glad that the equity bull run is continuing as high yields make life harder — and hoping very much that it can continue.

Survival Tips

It was very sad to read today of the death by suicide of RJ Mitte , at the age of 34. He was famous for playing Walter White Jr., son of one of the greatest anti-heroes in modern drama, in Breaking Bad . Mitte, like his character in the show, suffered from cerebral palsy , and his relationship with his parents as his father moves ever deeper into a life of extreme crime was always beautifully and movingly rendered.

Breaking Bad came top of the poll in the New York Times’ recent table of the best TV shows of this century , beating The Wire and Mad Men . When I asked readers which golden age drama I should binge while recovering from surgery a few years ago, it was far more widely recommended than even The Sopranos (which started in 1999 and wasn’t eligible for the Times’ list). I haven’t regretted watching every episode; it’s beautiful and in its twisted way suffused with genius. If you haven’t binge-watched it yet, you should. Mitte, as you can see here , here and here , was a vital member of a remarkable ensemble. Rest in peace, RJ.
