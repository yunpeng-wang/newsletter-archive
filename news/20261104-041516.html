It Looks Like a Blue Wave

Much remains to be decided in the US midterms at the time of writing (keep up with the latest results here ), but the main debate is whether this is a Blue Tsunami or just a Blue Big Wave.

Points of Return will have something detailed on this in another 24 hours. For now, the initial reaction is predictable. Nasdaq 100 eMini futures reacted very negatively to sweeping Democratic gains:

A Democratic Congress probably doesn’t mean more regulation, but could well hamper further deregulation. That explains startling selloffs for the Korean chipmakers Samsung Electronics Co. and SK Hynix Inc. (because of fears of AI regulation), and for Bitcoin (for which Republicans had just failed to roll back regulations). Gold is flourishing. This might produce buying opportunities, but not yet:

Longer term, the apparent scale of the Democratic Party’s win is a surprise. Few took the idea that Democrats would gain six Senate seats seriously, to judge by Polymarket’s contract for Republicans’ chances to win 47 seats or fewer .

Particularly if the Californian billionaire tax proposition passes, a radically redistributionist Democrat in 2028 will suddenly look far more plausible. The immediate financial effects of a Democratic sweep aren’t that great. Pricing in risks for 2028 that weren’t previously taken seriously, however, threatens to be a big deal.

Postcard From São Paulo

Greetings from Brazil’s extraordinary financial capital, where the hushed streets are filled with the hum of Chinese electric cars and buses, and nobody has ever heard of Tesla.

Investors have had a week to rethink their initial stunned Black Monday-level reaction to Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s shock victory in the second round of the presidential election. They still aren’t looking for bargains, and Brazilian assets continue to fall. The benchmark Bovespa is up for the year, in dollar terms, but only just:

Locals don’t want to hold equities in these circumstances while trading volume in the main exchange-traded fund tracking Brazilian equities shows intense activity by foreigners — who evidently aren’t seeing a bargain yet:

Perhaps most startling is the rise in inflation expectations. Mexico, widely treated as Brazil’s closest peer, is included for comparison:

Why such a reaction? If nothing else , Lula is a known factor and has done a great job of reducing Brazilian dependence on foreign financing. The country can now avoid a default by printing money and paying it to local investors. That’s why inflation expectations have surged while default risk — despite a bump in the last week — remains low compared to recent history.

Lula has a track record of doing what’s necessary to placate markets and avert a return to serious inflation, even if he hasn’t made the kind of profound reforms that might drive significant growth.

The reason markets have responded so negatively to Lula’s fourth term is, to quote one investor I met today, because “it’s different this time.” An ancient cliche holds that these are the most dangerous four words in finance, but he — and others — gave some good reasons why they think fourth time will be unlucky.

It starts with the ongoing problem that Brazil has a worse fiscal position than any of its Latin American peers (except Argentina, sadly an exception to every rule for many years). It improved under Lula in his first stint in power from 2003 to 2010, but now the International Monetary Fund sees gross debt topping 100% of gross domestic product by the end of his term:

Lula needs to rein this in, the São Paulo investment community believes, or Brazil will need to continue with exceptionally high rates, and bond yields. These attract the funds the government needs to finance its deficit and keep a lid on the inflation that might otherwise result from fiscal largesse — but they also crimp growth:

Lula was scrupulous about maintaining the central bank’s independence in his first stint in power. The bank, which meets after the market closes on Wednesday, had been slowly cutting the benchmark Selic rate from its recent 15% high. It’s now thought highly likely (about an 80% chance) to hold, and bolster support after this selloff.

If Lula wants to change this, investors believe, he has to pull back spending, or at least send an emphatic signal of his intent to do so — most obviously through his choice of finance secretary. Or, with six members of the nine-member central bank board to be appointed by the end of next year, he could go in the opposite direction and attempt to impinge on its independence.

Is this really likely? Tuesday’s resumed selloff was sparked by an innocuous report that the president would meet with Andre Lara Resende , a 75-year-old economist who has long been influential in Brazil. Resende published a paper over the weekend, titled J’Accuse: A Proposal to Eliminate Brazil’s Absurd Interest Rates . He argues:

Debt service amounts to more than 10% of GDP a year and is responsible for the rising and unsustainable trajectory of the debt-to-GDP ratio. Without cutting the interest rate to something around the nominal growth rate of the economy, no fiscal adjustment can halt the growth of debt relative to GDP.

So he says the arrow of causation runs from high rates to fiscal problems, not the other way around, and proposes a National Fiscal and Monetary Council to set five-year fiscal and monetary targets and preside over steady cuts in rates.

The piece is worth reading if you want to challenge priors you’ve probably held ever since you did Economics 101. Suffice it to say that it convinced nobody I met in Sao Paulo today or, judging by prices, anyone trading in the markets.

It was popular to draw the comparison with Turkey’s Recep Tayyip Erdogan, who thought that lower rates would reduce inflation and fired a series of central bank presidents until they did what he wanted in 2021. This is what happened:

The government has issued denials that Lula is planning rate cuts, or will be meeting Resende . The grim assumption is that he dislikes markets, but would react to a true bond market crisis — and that means São Paulo’s investors believe they will need to turn vigilante and force one. Until then, there’s no enthusiasm to go hunting for bargains.

Apple of the AI

Why have tech stocks hit an air pocket? It’s the earnings, stupid.

The world’s most valuable company, Apple Inc., delivered earnings that topped analysts’ expectations. It beat forecasts on every headline metric, including revenue, earnings per share, gross margin, operating income, and net income. That should be great news for the chief executive officer, John Ternus, in his first quarterly report since taking over from Tim Cook:

But market reaction suggests these beats alone were not enough . More important was the mixed outlook, which fed into uncertainty about AI returns. It expects sales growth to to slow in this quarter, producing a gross margin slightly below the 47% estimate. The decline of as much as 4% in after-hours trading suggests investors disliked the guidance:

While Apple has largely been spared the intense scrutiny directed at tech mega caps building out AI infrastructure, that outlook, and disappointing sales in China, gave other reasons for concern. An “unprecedented” rise in memory chip prices , according to Ternus, is also likely to weigh on margins, even with price adjustments. Still, investors may have overreacted to the margin squeeze, given the company’s light AI capital burden. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Robert Schiffman argues that this should preserve Apple’s strong credit profile:

With $145 billion of annual operating cash flow and debt falling to about $80 billion, Apple can absorb higher AI costs without external funding pressure.

Meanwhile, Intel Corp.’s plunge after its revenue forecast missed estimates showed yet again how little patience investors have for companies seeking to generate returns on AI. Persistent supply chain disruptions are hampering Intel’s ability to meet growing demand for its general-purpose chips amid the increasing deployment of AI services. That added up to a terrible October:

Despite recent pressure on mega caps to justify AI capex, a rally in a gauge tracking 55 US-listed stocks at the forefront of generative AI provided a breather on Tuesday by ticking up 1.84%:

As Points of Return noted here , the falls are driven by a valuation correction. This could have wider implications, as Dean Chen of Bitunix notes, if expanding computing capacity fails to generate corresponding cash flows. That could turn a promising investment for them into a source of funding pressure.

That’s still not a base case, but the way this season’s earnings have been greeted shows that investors are beginning to treat it as a possibility.
