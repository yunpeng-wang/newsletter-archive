France’s Deluge

Paris is burning and it’s terrifying to behold. Indeed, with riots in other cities , much of Europe is burning . Does it matter?

France is a much smaller share of the global economy than it used to be, and now accounts for less than 3% of global gross domestic product, less than half its level from 1980. It matters less than it once did to the rest of the world:

There’s also some evidence that France’s fiscal woes aren’t driving contagion to other euro zone members in the way that happened during the crisis of 2010-12. If anything, it’s US Treasuries, rather than French OATS, that appear most contagious. Spreads of Italian and Greek bonds over German bunds widened sharply during September, when Treasury yields were surging — although they always lagged the sharp rise in French yields. So far this month, increasing French spreads in response to the scenes on the streets have been accompanied by narrowing premiums for Greece and Italy:

The euro zone crisis’ acute contagion stemmed from banks, who were heavily exposed to peripheral countries’ debt. That is no longer the case, and this chart from Absolute Strategy Research shows that while French bank stocks have tanked during this crisis, others in the region have not:

It thus makes some sense that US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent averred Tuesday that French problems wouldn’t spill over elsewhere. In a highly connected global financial system, the risk of contagion is always present, but in this case, Bessent argued, “The French have a tough hand, but President Macron’s economic team, whom I know very well, are really first rate.”

But greater issues remain. Whatever Bessent’s opinion, investors have lost confidence in Team Macron as the president nears the end of his 10 years in power. He was feted for stopping a third populist black swan after Brexit and Trump when he beat the far right’s Marine Le Pen for the presidency in 2017. But his disastrously misjudged snap legislative elections in 2024, losing what control his centrists still retained, brought French spreads over bunds back to their worst levels from the 2017 campaign. They’ve now ballooned far higher:

With investors’ alarm growing, Ian Harnett of Absolute Strategy argues that contagion risks are “likely to revolve around the 57.5% of French government debt held by foreign institutions, not banks.” Insurers , in particular, could be an issue. By comparison, only 35.9% of Italian BTPs are foreign owned.

Further, the room for either Macron or his successor to maneuver is limited. At nearly 110% of GDP, French government debt is similar to the US (while the rest of the euro zone has been more stringent):

The problem is that France’s private sector is already leveraged to the hilt in a way that far outstrips the US or the rest of Europe. That means that the country cannot readily raise more debt by selling to its own private sector:

Markets outside France are treating this with relative calm because of the experience of 2010-2012, when the European Central Bank famously intervened. Jonas Goltermann of Capital Economics argues:

The strengthening of the euro zone’s institutional framework and the improvement of ECB’s crisis-fighting toolset over the past decade and a half provides some comfort that France’s fiscal problems are still a long way from sparking a full-scale re-run of the 2010-12 crisis .

But French politics will still ensure that the situation gets worse before it improves. Goltermann takes the Italian political crisis of 2018, when an incoherent coalition of left- and right-populists attempted a fight with Brussels over the deficit, as a template. Italian yields’ spread over German bunds topped at 325 basis points before the government backtracked.

Investors think they see how this ends — but the drama still needs to play out in the streets and ballot boxes, and in insurers’ balance sheets.

Housing Gloom Thickens

Mortgage rates aren’t that high. Four decades ago, they were in double-digits and made the American Dream of home-ownership prohibitively expensive. But for a generation accustomed to being able to fund housing purchases at rates near 4%, the surge in rates over the last five years has had much the same effect. Pending home sales , tracking signed contracts for which transactions have not closed, plunged last month to their lowest level since the National Association of Realtors began keeping the numbers in 2001:

Is this a cyclical response to rising borrowing costs, or a more meaningful breakdown in housing affordability? Perhaps a combination, since these aren’t mutually exclusive. Bankrate.com’s national average for a 30-year fixed mortgage, which follows rates available to borrowers at the application stage, recently topped its highest level since 2024 . The benchmark Fannie Mae 30-year bond yield, tracking the market where such mortgages are financed, is more sensitive to the Fed’s policy trajectory. It has declined this month, but is still hovering near a three-year high:

This surge in mortgage rates, driven primarily by the Treasury selloff, offers the most plausible explanation for the housing market drying up. It simply makes homes less affordable. While the slight pullback in Treasury yields should halt the upward march in mortgage rates, Thomas Ryan of Capital Economics argues that a more sustained decline is needed to repair sales activity in any meaningful way:

As a result, we expect sales to remain very weak through the end of the year, with transactions unlikely to rise above the 4 million mark. Next year should bring some improvement, as lower energy prices and a slightly less hawkish Fed tightening cycle than investors currently anticipate help to ease some of the pressure.

How soon this unravels depends on what’s driving the slump. Oxford Economics’ Bernard Yaros says it’s no longer possible to attribute it to people deferring their demand. Unlike in past cycles when housing slows this badly, the Fed at this point is more likely to hike rates than cut them, as the spending on the AI buildout is totally out of sync with the state of the real estate market. Homebuyers and sellers cannot expect cheaper mortgages to rescue them, at least in the near term.

Lower rates will only be possible if inflation — propped up by tariffs, geopolitics, and AI investment demand — trends clearly toward the Fed’s target:

Some move toward fiscal rectitude would help bring down long-term interest rates, but the window for fiscal reforms isn’t until early next decade, when Social Security’s trust fund will run out of reserves.

That would be a long time for the housing market to stay becalmed. Building permits issued also fell last week, reinforcing the weak demand narrative. But there is more nuance beyond the headlines. September’s fall was driven by a decrease in single-family permits, while those for multifamily units shot up. The data are strongly seasonal, and the more reliable three-month moving average suggests that activity is not slowing down anything like so badly:

Barclays’ Colin Johanson notes that the trend reflects persistent weakness as the double whammy of high rates and elevated prices weigh on buyers. Sluggish activity also hurts homebuilders. The S&P 1500 Homebuilding index is down more than 10% year to date, and almost back to the nadir after last year’s Liberation Day tariffs:

Demand destruction from high rates has much to do with this, but it’s only a part of the story. Carl Weinberg of High Frequency Economics argues that building materials such as lumber, steel, copper, and aluminum, made costlier by tariffs and prone to unpredictable swings, are compounding the problem. Skilled labor is also in short supply as a surge in data-center construction competes for the same workers:

Even though the supply of houses for sale is short, don’t expect a rapid recovery in homebuilding until inflation uncertainty is addressed, monetary policy uncertainty is removed, bond yields stop rising, and corrosive uncertainty about costs, tariffs, and other policies is resolved. With the population declining, no fundamental pulse is driving demand for new homes anyway.

Even a prompt rate cut wouldn’t turn housing into an economic motor. The broader economy is strong despite weakening consumer sentiment, but Jay Hatfield of Infrastructure Capital Advisors argues that further declines in housing activity triggered by Fed tightening could drive a recession. For now, the AI boom may shield the economy from the effects of further hikes.

The greatest risk then becomes that rates rise enough to shut off the high-yield market for companies trying to finance AI infrastructure. Whatever happens, housing is unlikely to boost the economy any time soon; as ever, the focus has to stay on AI.
