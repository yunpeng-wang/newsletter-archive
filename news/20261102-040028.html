Snoozing the AI Buzz

Every selloff of tech stocks chips away at the vaunted promise of artificial intelligence — and there have been several. Last week’s reaction to third-quarter earnings, which saw Alphabet Inc. alone lose more than $530 billion in market cap, brings the day of reckoning a step closer. Whether this one differs from previous selloffs and signals an enduring erosion of investor confidence depends on the underlying issues and whether they can be resolved.

The magnitude this time is hard to ignore, but it remains highly concentrated. The spread between returns on the equal-weighted and cap-weighted versions of the S&P 500 was wider in October than in any month since April 2009, when stocks rebounded after the Global Financial Crisis:

Comparing the S&P 500 Equal Weight, where each stock accounts for 0.2% of the index regardless of market cap, with the Nasdaq 100, home of the tech titans, offers the clearest picture of the disappointment caused by the latest earnings. In previous selloffs since the launch of ChatGPT ignited the AI bull market, broad indices have also fallen. This time is very different, with the average stock notching gains, even as the Nasdaq falls 9% from its peak:

Such a divergence suggests it isn’t macro- or rate-driven, but a direct and targeted repricing of the AI trade itself, from which the rest of the economy is deemed to be effectively decoupling. The Nasdaq 100 has endured a big derating of its price/earnings multiple, in an effect masked by rising earnings, so this looks a lot like a correction of tech’s oft-cited overvaluation:

How far will this go from here? Based on valuation, historical pullbacks, and technical levels, Keith Lerner of Truist Wealth suggests potential downsides of from 2% to 8% for the tech sector from current levels. Valuation provides the most compelling support. When the bull market began in October 2022, the S&P 500 Information Technology sector’s forward price-to-earnings ratio was 18.2 times. Applying that multiple to current forward earnings estimates implies a price level near 6,600, about 2% under current levels. Other factors could take the index below this floor:

The key risk is earnings estimates. Over the past three months, tech’s forward earnings estimates have risen approximately 17%, more than double the increase for the S&P 500.

If valuation is encouraging, historical performance is not. Lerner suggests the current pullback could have a further 8% to run to match the average decline in the five previous tech selloffs during this bull market, which saw losses ranging from 10% to 26%. The adjustment may not be over, particularly as elevated yields weigh on multiples. At the same time, earnings misses, as seen at Intel Corp., Alphabet Inc. and Amazon.com Inc., undermine the profit outlook underpinning these valuations.

Bloomberg Economics’ Anna Wong flags three additional risks to the AI trade: converging frontier-model capabilities could erode pricing power; permitting, energy and regulatory constraints could slow capex; and more rate hikes could raise discount-rates:

Another leg higher in rates, combined with weaker AI revenues and tighter credit, would change the picture. That’s the combination that could turn today’s financial wobble into tomorrow’s investment bust.

So far, this selloff has coincided with falling rate expectations since the decline in Treasuries hit bottom early last month. These things are at least partly related — concerns about access to financing introduced a new element of uncertainty.

Whether a return to monetary tightening — still very likely in December — could deliver a decisive blow to the AI trade is harder to gauge. As Capital Economics’ Thomas Ryan points out, the equity market and broader financial conditions will be important considerations for the Federal Reserve.

Meanwhile, the key dynamic in the post-ChatGPT era has been to bet on tech-led growth, which means buying tech stocks relative to bonds. The Nasdaq’s advance over Treasuries, proxied in the chart by the most popular exchange-traded funds tracking the sectors, has been remarkably consistent. September’s bond selloff took this trade beyond its long-term trend a month ago. After a few weeks of brutal correction, that trend remains intact:

The two most significant interruptions to this upward trend to date have come with the ghastly policy errors of the Liberation Day tariffs and then the attack on Iran. This incident, for now, is not as serious. Further deterioration from here would be meaningful.

—Richard Abbey

Midterms

Voters can surprise us. Polls and prediction markets can be wrong. Electorates in Brazil and Serbia made that clear in the last week. When elections spring a surprise, financial market reactions can be drastic, as shown by the selloff that greeted the reelection of Brazil’s President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva .

Bear all this in mind as we approach Tuesday’s election night in the US. If the polls and the prediction markets have this right, the voters are about to give President Donald Trump and his party a serious kicking. This is how the odds of a clean sweep, with Democrats taking control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, have moved on the two biggest prediction markets, Polymarket and Kalshi:

Democrats’ chances of a Blue Wave are 70% — which is still less than the 80% shot Flavio Bolsonaro appeared to have against Lula in Brazil a week ago, but it still makes sense to call this a base case because they have a number of routes to their target.

For those keeping score on election night, the Democrats need to win a net four Senate seats, and Polymarket bettors think they’ll get six. In order of confidence, they are North Carolina (98%), Ohio (74%), Alaska (74%), Texas (71%), Maine (68%), and Kansas (52%). Dems look more likely to win Iowa (42%) or even Nebraska (where an independent is on 27%), than to lose their most vulnerable state, Michigan (a 29% shot for the Republicans). You can entertain yourself by checking the latest odds on Polymarket’s website here .

Such a blue wave would bring many certainties of the Trump decade into question. But with the exception of North Carolina, none are priced as certainties, and much depends on individual candidates. Look only at the ructions in Michigan (where Democrats’ choice of Abdul El-Sayed has split the party and gives the GOP a chance) or Texas, where Democrats are buoyed by Republicans’ extraordinary nomination of the state’s scandal-ridden attorney general, Ken Paxton. Both results matter; neither tells us much about the future:

How much does all this matter to markets? The president is in place for another two years, and there is no chance of putting together a majority that could override his veto. Trump’s big tax cut for his second term has already happened, and he would veto any attempt to push through a hike.

A big Democratic win, coupled with victory for the billionaire tax proposal in California , would suggest the party might just win in 2028 with a candidate committed to raising taxes . That would be market-negative (to put it mildly); but Democrats would still need to choose a candidate and win the election (both difficult), so in the near-term this should do little more than affect the vibe.

As for the budget, Christian Keller of Barclays see two scenarios:

The first is that bipartisan dealmaking is precluded, leaving Congress to pass a series of continuing resolutions to fund the government. The second, a scenario with bipartisan dealmaking, would likely result in more spending, as was the case during the previous Trump administration, when spending increased roughly 8% from FY18 to FY19 after Democrats won the House .

Democrats do seem threatening on the issue of AI regulation. But the president is an outspoken fan and holds a veto over legislation. The risk is more at the local and state level, as Libby Cantrill of Pimco explains :

The politics around data centers are just so bad, and policymakers are going to be responding. Lots of these gubernatorial candidates are running on an anti-data center moratorium. They’re advocating for a full moratorium. For lenders like Pimco, we’re just being a lot more selective in terms of the deals that we are considering, and really paying attention to the politics.

Congress cannot shift the market calculus significantly until the 2028 presidential election. A blue wave this week would change little in the short term, even if it shifted the odds and therefore the market prices for the longer term. A true surprise would be much more interesting. The odds on a Republican sweep are below 10% (lower than Lula’s last week):

If the GOP somehow held the House, it would be a massive vote of confidence in Trumpism and empower a radical end to his time in office. This hasn’t been priced in at all.

Barclays suggests that another tax cut would then be likely — and that would likely intensify both the rush to buy growth stocks and the upward pressure on inflation and interest rates. It would also be bullish for the dollar — the DXY dollar index has rallied after Trump victories over the last decade, and stalled after his defeats and setbacks, as Mizuho Financial illustrates:

The likelihood is that a strong night for Democrats causes a mild freak-out over events that cannot happen for another two years. The slim Lula-level possibility of a last Trump hurrah would have much more impact.

Survival Tips

After Kevin Warsh made us all wait until December for another rate hike, we have a number of new songs on the theme of waiting and patience. There’s Paula Cole’s I Don’t Want to Wait , Sittin’ on the Dock of the Bay by Otis Redding, Foreigner’s Waiting for a Girl Like You , Tired of Waiting for You by The Kinks, Waiting for the Men in Black by The Stranglers, I’m Waiting for the Man by the Velvet Underground, Working and Waiting by The Clash, Wait & See by Hikaru Utada and Waiting by The Cure. Perhaps Gillian Welch’s much-nominated Look at Miss Ohio captures Warsh’s spirit best: “You wanna do right, but not right now.”
