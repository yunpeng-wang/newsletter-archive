A Yen for Appreciation

For decades, the world of finance has grown used to treating Japan as the exception to all rules. While the rest of the world surged and collapsed, Japan trudged on with minimal interest rates and sluggish growth for decades. It was a safe place to borrow money cheap, through any number of elaborate trades. Beyond that, it was a world unto itself. Psychologically, at least, it was almost as if Commodore Perry ’s work had been undone, and Japan had closed to the world once more.

That attitude is being forcibly changed. The yen is historically weak after so many years of being used as the rest of the world’s source of cheap cash. The Japanese authorities intervened twice this year to strengthen it, the second time with the active support of the US Treasury. And both times, after an impressive shift, the yen soon weakened.

Last week, the dollar suddenly dropped against the yen in a move comparably dramatic to those interventions, and so far there has been no correction. Notably, there wasn’t an official attempt to prod the currency higher by buying bonds. This time, it seems, a stronger yen is for real:

Gavekal’s Anatole Kaletsky describes the yen as “more undervalued than any major currency in modern history.” Last week’s recovery, he pointed out, came “despite (or because of) a very strong US payroll report that in the past would virtually have guaranteed a weakening yen and strengthening dollar.” His positive take was:

The yen is finally freeing itself from its longstanding slavery to interest differentials and instead is starting to respond to relative economic conditions in Japan and the US. The most important of these — relative prices, current accounts, fiscal policies and inflationary prospects — all imply a much stronger yen, especially against the dollar but also against the euro, pound and even the Swiss franc.

And indeed, the yen started at an absurdly cheap level, and it remains ridiculously weak. This is how it has performed on a real (taking into account that Japanese inflation has been the lowest in the world, which would normally mean the currency should strengthen) effective basis against a broad basket of currencies:

But why exactly is this happening now, and without desperate prodding from the US or Japanese authorities? This looks like a genuine and important market signal, but it’s come with no spending of governmental firepower a month after a massive US-Japan intervention. We now know that involved the biggest monthly decline in Japanese foreign exchange reserves on record, but it had a relatively muted effect:

The key lies in the movement of huge sums of money. The yen was not only very cheap when this episode started; it was also being bet against to a reckless degree. Once some volatility returned, plenty of traders were exposed and had an incentive to reduce their shorts — which meant that the currency would recover. As Masayuki Nakajima of Mizuho illustrates, leveraged funds were very short at the beginning of this month, while the August intervention had still left other investors with hefty vulnerable positions:

In such conditions, it was not difficult to start a move. Last week’s news that Norway’s Norges Fund, one of the biggest sovereign wealth pools, was reallocating its fixed income portfolio in a way that likely shifts from Treasuries to Japanese bonds prompted speculation that more international money would move back to Tokyo and its newly competitive yields.

Jesper Koll, who publishes the Japan Optimist newsletter, points out that the rise in bond yields has left the country’s biggest quasi-national asset managers (known as the whales ) nursing losses that might force them to make sales — even though they’re still sitting on big profits from overseas investments. This would be the first forced selling since 2011 (which was triggered by FX losses). He said:

This “implosion risk” is what the Treasury secretary is worried about when he says Japan is a key provider of global capital and weakness there will have spillover effects that we must guard against.

With Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi determined to put savings to work at home, and the US prepared to help, big institutions are listening to her. If it’s one of the Japanese entities switching from dollars into yen, the move could have a lot further to run.

Beyond the whales, the currency also depends on the Bank of Japan, which is expected to raise rates next week. Overnight rates are now forecast to go up by a full percentage point over the next 12 months, to 1.9%, following a shift in perception of the economy.

The country’s post-pandemic inflation surge has been difficult to judge because it was exaggerated by a bad harvest for rice . Core inflation, excluding food and energy, is significantly higher than it’s been in three decades (the two spikes visible were both created by consumption tax hikes), but well below 2%:

On this basis, it’s possible to argue that the BOJ should wait a little longer to hike rates. But market-based inflation expectations have stepped higher, with five-year breakevens above 2% for the past year. Japan is now expected to operate by the same inflationary rules as anyone else:

Perhaps critically, real earnings are starting to rise, while nominal wage growth is the strongest in decades — as illustrated by Mizuho Financial Group:

In these circumstances, higher rates might indeed head off inflation rather than strangling another attempt at growth.

Instead of hoping for a global slowdown, the strategy under Japan’s control would be to speed up repatriation of assets from overseas. If that happens, the rest of the world will need to learn how to do without Japanese funding.

ECB Walking Alone

The European Central Bank is walking the lone path as the only G7 central bank on a tightening cycle. A 25-basis-point hike is all but guaranteed for Thursday as the energy crisis forces a second consecutive hike. With no resolution to the conflict in sight, price pressures will likely remain elevated, leaving investors to parse the meeting for clues about next steps. For now, the consensus is that the Governing Council will not hike again after September.

With the Federal Reserve meeting next week, any guidance from ECB President Christine Lagarde will gain more significance. It’s not yet the base case that the Fed follows Europe down the tightening path — but it does seem clear, for all President Donald Trump’s complaints , that the Fed’s easing cycle is over. Oxford Economics’ Oliver Rakau argues:

Our view is that the ECB is done hiking after next week’s move. The main reason is that we think that underlying inflation pressures will be contained enough that there won’t be another council majority to be found for a hike.

For now, the direction of policy rests on growth, Europe’s labor market, and energy prices. The worst-case scenario of higher fuel prices crimping growth has not yet materialized. Rakau points out that solid growth and a resilient labor market lower the bar for further tightening:

Still, the latest surge in gas and electricity prices entails upside risks to the bank’s updated inflation forecasts. Headline inflation has moved further from target, but Mizuho’s Leejun Foo notes that core inflation remains contained and price rises for services have continued to ease:

The LNG and gas story sits at the center of this debate. Our conclusion is that rates markets are already pricing something close to the ECB’s adverse energy scenario... investors are paying up for protection against winter scarcity risk rather than pricing a permanent structural supply shortage.

Overnight index swaps, meanwhile, still price an eventual rise to 3% by the end of next year, according to Bloomberg World Interest Rate Probabilities:

A 3% rate is hard to justify when there is no evidence of second-round effects, and implies belief in continuing higher energy prices — which would likely choke off growth. But Deutsche Bank’s Mark Wall suggests that the AI buildout might raise the neutral rate ( known as r* ):

But we see a way to rationalize the market-implied terminal rate. First, r* rises amid the global AI investment boom, reinforcing the boost to equilibrium rates from Germany’s defense and infrastructure spending. Second, energy prices are held higher by persistent demand from AI investment (data centers).

Ultimately, given the balance of risks, a pragmatic approach from Lagarde might involve sounding tough on inflation without pre-committing to further tightening. She is likely to face questions about her own future , and about the rise of the far right in Germany — but the market response will depend on what she says about the ECB’s reaction function in an uncertain environment.
