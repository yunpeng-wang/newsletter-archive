Choose Your Analogy

New Century Financial’s bankruptcy was a big deal. When it went bust on April 2, 2007, it was the largest subprime mortgage lender ever to fail, overwhelmed by defaults from borrowers who should never have received a loan in the first place. Its creditors, led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, claimed almost $35 billion in subsequent court proceedings.

Sixteen years later, the closure of Silicon Valley Bank also appeared a huge deal. Caught out by the sharp fall in Treasury prices , it had some $110 billion in assets when regulators closed it on March 10, 2023. Like New Century, it had enjoyed stratospheric growth, reaching a market cap of more than $40 billion.

After both incidents, the S&P 500 shuffled sideways for six months. And both seem similar to the current situation at the computing provider CoreWeave. It went public last year, does business at the absolute center of the artificial intelligence trade, and confidence in its credit is in freefall:

Its yield ticked down slightly on Monday, but the parallels remain evident. It’s in the business of the moment, and Treasury yields have surged as they did ahead of New Century and SVB. Is the entire AI trade is about to fall apart?

Not necessarily. The broader response to those earlier crises (which finished their respective companies, a fate that hasn’t befallen CoreWeave) diverged in a big way. Both looked like CoreWeave in real time. The question is whether this is more SVB (S&P 500 up 40% over the next two years) or New Century (the S&P fell 40% in two years):

With hindsight, it’s bizarre that evidence that subprime was collapsing could be ignored so long, and that SVB caused much greater fear in real time than New Century. Rate expectations collapsed while the VIX equity volatility index spiked higher in 2023 than in 2007:

In hindsight, SVB was an overdone panic swiftly resolved, but traders extrapolated the problems of a small group of regional banks to the entire banking system. For now, CoreWeave looks similar.

Brian Stutland of Equity Armor Investments argues that CoreWeave’s issues center on its own financial business model, rather than the case for AI as a whole. The key question:

Is their business model profitable, and is it profitable when interest rates go higher and the cost of borrowing gets too expensive for them to build out a data center in which the revenue pledges that are being made by all these names make sense in terms of the cost to build versus the profitability of the revenue coming in?

Stutland calls the answer a “coin flip.” But this isn’t a New Century moment that reveals a huge systemic risk. Barclays’ Ajay Rajadhyaksha pointed out that CoreWeave’s unsecured bonds, currently under attack, are less than $2 billion of a total debt stack of about $35 billion, and are “structurally subordinated to everything else.” They might be rationally priced for their own prospects, he contends, but say little or nothing about the viability of the great AI buildout. The analogy appears to be SVB, not New Century.

Plainly, the AI capex cycle is comparably important to the subprime boom. The price of computing capacity, Rajadhyaksha contends, will decide if it shares subprime’s fate:

CoreWeave’s business model — and the business model of every AI infrastructure company — is a spread business. Buy GPUs, rent them out, pocket the difference between the rental yield and the cost of financing. That spread holds as long as demand for compute exceeds supply and rental rates stay high enough to cover debt service, depreciation, and operating costs.

Evidence that those rents aren’t as high as hoped might unleash a true crisis. CoreWeave is an alarming situation that provides no evidence on this.

Meanwhile, in France...

As predicted, French bonds took a pounding on Monday after a weekend of violence. At 4.933%, the French 10-year government bond is at its highest yield since July 2002 (even if it is still lower than the 10-year US Treasury, driven by a far stronger American economy). Its spread over the equivalent German bund rose by 10.3 basis points to 1.48%, its highest since 2012. In March, it stood at only 0.58%.

Much now depends on rating agencies, with Moody’s — whose rating is currently two notches above those of S&P and Fitch — expected to make a review by the end of the week. A downgrade should have no practical effect, but might well impact psychology at a difficult moment.

Psychology is also affected by the chances of Jean-Luc Melenchon, the far-left presidential candidate who has floated reneging on French debt — a move that would obviously be very market-unfriendly. A Melenchon victory cannot be ruled out, particularly if he finds himself in a second round run-off with the far-right’s ascendant Marine Le Pen. But it’s unlikely, and his prediction market odds are exactly where they started the year. The violence, which so far has been laid at the door of the far left, may have dented his chances:

The situation is deeply concerning. Market psychology for now may matter more than anything President Emmanuel Macron can do , or the mood on the streets of France.

Red-Hot Copper

Copper has traded sideways for months, but a confluence of structural factors make it hard to dislodge from near all-time highs . Its integral use in the data-center buildout and energy transition underpins demand; challenges at major mines worldwide further prop up prices. Likely US copper tariffs have prompted stockpiling, while Chinese demand continues to play a part.

Put these factors together and in four years copper has risen nearly 80%:

With so many diffuse factors in play, it’s hard to gauge how much further this can run. The tariff premium has lifted prices in New York, but almost midway through Donald Trump’s second term, levies are yet to materialize. Doubts are growing whether they will ever see the light of day. That’s translated into sideways pricing action:

The midterms now become a point of risk. Prices could crash toward $10,000 a ton if a Democratic victory forces the president to rethink, according to Macquarie. Or they could rally to $18,000 if tariffs are announced, with a lag that lets traders import even more metal.

Copper imports have surged ahead of the anticipated tariffs. As BCA Research shows, this has driven a major dislocation in global inventories, with US exchanges’ stockpiles ballooning to 15 times their 2020-2024 average:

BCA suggests that the Trump administration’s curious reluctance to impose levies on copper is not accidental. The US cannot easily or quickly boost its copper production, leaving it with no choice but to keep importing:

That would mean higher costs for the US manufacturing sector, which is likely to be viewed unfavorably — especially against the current backdrop of energy-driven inflation risks.

Meanwhile, structural supply and demand forces should offset any weakness if the US abandons its tariffs. Harry Colvin of Longview Economics argues that copper faces a structural deficit that will drive higher long-term prices. He estimates a 1.7 million-ton cumulative supply deficit through 2028, compared with about 1 million tons in London Metal Exchange (LME), COMEX, and Shanghai inventories. It’s hard to deal with this shortfall, as many current mines have been in operation for a while and their efficiency is deteriorating :

You’ve got tight supply, old assets, complicated operations, having to move much more rock to get the same amount of metal out, and things going wrong more because there’s not been enough capex. And then you’ve got the tight fundamentals to sow the seeds for the next uptrend.

BloombergNEF forecasts global demand of 36 million metric tons by 2035, much of it for the energy transition — and potentially a 9 million-ton shortfall. Liz Dennett of mining technology group Endolith argues: “We need more copper between now and 2050 than in the history of human civilization, and that demand is only getting higher.”

New mines take years to open, but without improved productivity, only a collapse in demand would bring down prices. If the AI buildout stalls, demand for other uses in the emerging world would remain. Dr. Copper’s growing indispensability means prices have further room to run. Any pullback would most likely create a buying opportunity.

Survival Tips

There is joy in a goal from the halfway line . Over the weekend, Diego Gomez of my hometown team Brighton, scored this goal against rivals Crystal Palace, with his back foot planted in his own half. It’s the Premier League’s longest-range goal since this one by Wayne Rooney against West Ham in 2017. The most famous, also against Palace, launched David Beckham toward legendary status in 1996 (the commentator at the time instantly said it was a “a goal you’ll see over and over again”). Czechia’s Patrick Schick embarrassed Scotland with this one in the 2021 European Championships . One of Pele’s most famous World Cup moments was a failed attempt to do the same thing against Uruguay in 1970.

They’re implausible and exciting moments, which by definition spring out of nowhere. They require immense skill and vision, embarrass the goalie, and need a lot of luck. I’ve no idea how this can be measured, but Gomez’s shot had only a 1% chance of scoring when it left his boot, apparently. As he was in a tight position with several opponents closing in, while most of the others were scored by players who had been given way too much space, I don’t think it’s just my bias that gives his effort higher marks than some others (particularly Beckham’s). But in general, do we overrate the spectacular long-range shot? And are there equivalents in other sports? ( Steph Curry in basketball is the best one I can think of , particularly as he generally executes his longest-range three-pointers under intense time pressure, but he makes it too routine to be so exciting.)
