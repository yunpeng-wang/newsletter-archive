France Is Burning

The mayor of the ancient French town of Limoges has inveighed against a “failure of the state” after rioters burned down the historic city hall on Saturday, the worst day of street violence France had witnessed in years. The chances are that voters will agree when they choose a new president next year. Markets could come to a verdict much more quickly.

Prediction market bettors seem to assume that scenes of disorder will help the far-right Rassemblement Nationale led by Marine Le Pen. Her chances are their highest ever, although still slightly below 50%:

That political uncertainty is unhelpful. Markets responded positively to rightward moves in Latin American elections over the last year, but the central political debate these days isn’t on a left-right spectrum, and advances for Le Pen (and similar politicians elsewhere in Europe) have been greeted with caution. Eric Robertsen of Standard Chartered PLC argued:

Perhaps it is not the expected rightward shift, per se, that is causing angst in all things euro. Perhaps it is the fear that more extreme political parties will pursue more radical and populist agendas that are not economically or fiscally sound. Elections are always a referendum of sorts, but in France especially, it appears that the 2027 election will be a referendum on the political establishment from both sides of the aisle.

The violence came at a point when bond market sentiment appeared to have stabilized after a sharp rise in the spread demanded for buying French bonds compared to German bunds. After two weeks of relative calm, this measure will be the focus of attention, particularly for the currency market:

Kit Juckes of Societe Generale SA commented that “at the time of writing I struggle to see how the euro absorbs mass protests against any form of fiscal austerity.” It’s difficult to disagree. When politicians are squeezed between voters demanding more spending, and bond markets insisting on less, a blow to confidence in the euro seems inevitable.

Moves in currency futures, even before the shocking images from Limoges and elsewhere, add to the reasons for concern. Juckes said:

The CFTC reported another steep increase in net long USD positions in the week to last Monday, alongside the biggest net euro short position since January 2016 and the aftermath of Mario Draghi’s move to cut rates to zero.

Yield differentials are a critical driver of exchange rates, and the euro gained a respite from last week’s downward turn in Treasury yields. There is a risk that political concerns will swamp this:

The mairie at Limoges appears to be gone forever, and beyond the chance of the kind of resurrection enjoyed by Notre-Dame cathedral. The same is not true of French finances. But the loss of control of the streets at this juncture increases the chances that markets will bring matters to a head before voters have their say.

When the Chips Are Down

The chipmakers are down, but certainly not yet out. Among a clutch of market reversals last week, the SOX index of US semiconductors still caught the eye. It remains up almost 60% for the year, but testing a level it has touched twice before in the last three months. For technical traders, this looks like a moment of truth is at hand:

The concentration on the technology names that have led recent advances is evident. The SOX’s fall has overlapped with a return to form for consumer staples stocks, at the opposite end of the risk spectrum. Ahead of third-quarter earnings season, it certainly looks as though investors are having cold feet about rosy estimates and moving somewhere safer:

But it’s not just about a considered reappraisal of the AI-induced tech rally. The turn in the Nasdaq 100 overlaps almost perfectly with the turn in the 10-year Treasury yield. The few weeks when investors appeared to be exiting bonds and substituting stocks have gone into reverse. Previous sharp rises in bond yields have tended to resolve when investors decide that Treasuries’ value is compelling and take profits in whatever had been hot — see emerging market stocks in 1994, subprime mortgage credit in 2007. That discomfiting narrative could fit again:

But why would this be the point to tip? US midterm elections are approaching and voters look likely to give President Donald Trump a bloody nose. There are ways this could be market-negative, but it’s hard to attribute this reversal to politics. The main prediction markets put the chance of Democratic control of the Senate at a little above 60% — where they’ve stood for several weeks:

Instead of the usual suspects, such as the Fed, it looks like markets are responding to the sound of a dog that had not previously barked — the credit market. As Simeon Hyman of ProShares showed in this chart, more or less the entire rise in investment grade bond yields for the year had been driven by the shift in Treasuries as of the end of September. The rise in the extra spread to compensate for risk was minimal — and was also very much contained for riskier high-yield of “junk” bonds:

This is a strange state of affairs, and Hyman suggested that the splurge of AI issuance, generally regarded as very safe, might explain it:

Typically, a surge in corporate debt issuance would lead to wider corporate credit spreads — the yield over and above “risk free” Treasury rates — as issuers compete with one another. In the current environment, however, massive corporate issuance may actually be pushing the “risk free” Treasury rates higher while leaving credit spreads alone .

That is changing. Bloomberg’s measure of US junk bond spreads is not at all extreme, but it’s risen sharply in the last couple of weeks without an obvious external shock to drive it:

The culprit, inevitably, is the AI trade. Alexander Altmann of Barclays Equity Technical Strategies offers this chart of the spreads payable on debt issued by five companies to finance building data centers. It’s impressive how narrow those spreads have been, but it’s clear that perceived risk has risen sharply:

Several different situations are driving those spreads higher, but CoreWeave , a data center operator that went public last year, is garnering the most attention. Yields of 15% on its debt show acute concern over whether it is sustainable.

Treasury yields catalyzed this concern. But the immediate future of the AI trade, and with it the long rally in US stocks, will depend on whether growing concerns in the credit market about AI debt can be allayed.

Freight Out of Hormuz

Attacks on commercial cargo ships in the Strait of Hormuz underscore that the disruption to the waterway goes beyond the energy crisis. Crude oil is the most useful barometer of the unfolding supply chain crisis caused by the Iran conflict, but the damage to non-oil shipping from mines or drones amid the US blockade are fast increasing the pain. Cargo vessels have been as badly affected as tankers:

The collapse in commercial cargo crossings is particularly striking. The brief activity spike after June’s 90-day ceasefire serves as a painful reminder to shippers how crucial it is to return to normalcy. Shipping costs have accumulated as the disruption continues, though they remain below their peaks after the pandemic:

The risk remains that higher costs become the new normal (and contribute to pricier finished goods). The price surge is driven mainly by the increased number of tankers needed for shuttling trade through the Persian Gulf, and by the growing risks from Iranian attacks. A key benchmark of daily earnings for supertankers on the Middle East-to-China route stood at $1.37 million on Thursday, a more than sixfold increase since the war started:

Record diesel prices have added another layer of pressure on freight rates, creating a feedback loop that threatens to increase consumer prices. Several import-sensitive components of US inflation, such as apparel and consumer appliances, have already posted sharp increases following outright deflation in 2024 and 2025:

For businesses, the pain has already arrived. The prices-paid component of September’s services survey from the Institute for Supply Management saw the highest reading in more than four years . This survey proved to be a strong early warning sign of the last inflation spike:

How much cost pressure can be attributed to freight and logistics? Bank of America’s Taylor Bowley points out that the bank’s proprietary data show a disproportionate burden on small businesses. Growth in small-business payments for freight and logistics surged to nearly 30% in June before moderating to 12.7% year-over-year in September, nearly four times more than mid-sized firms:

Bowley argues that smaller firms are typically less capable of absorbing rising logistics costs:

Larger companies also often benefit from dedicated logistics contracts and greater purchasing power, while smaller ones may be more exposed to spot-market transportation costs and fluctuations in freight pricing.

Bloomberg Intelligence’s Stephane Kovatchev expects freight rates to stay elevated through the end of the year. Next year is less certain. New ship deliveries and a fuller return to the Suez Canal could free up capacity and put downward pressure on rates. Still, the strong outlook is yet to be reflected in shippers’ operating margins compared to their post-pandemic fortunes:

For now, this is good for logistics stocks. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Lee Klaskow is forecasting that Maersk, the industry leader, will benefit from elevated freight rates, resilient demand, and persistent port congestion in its third-quarter earnings due next month. Ultimately, resolving the conflict would ease the price pressures. But escalation that involves the Houthis disrupting other transit points could hurt supply chains further, sending the pain further downstream for consumers.
