Postcard From Buenos Aires

Greetings from the hushed Argentine capital, which has come to a halt to welcome Pope Leo XIV . The pontiff was greeted on Sunday by the hobbit-like President Javier Milei, who has been in office since late 2023 and faces the electorate a year from now.

Argentina has a reputation for economic and financial drama, which Milei accentuated during the chainsaw-wielding campaign that brought him to power. His actual time in office has been oddly calm by such standards, and the 2027 election looks finely poised. Prediction markets saw his reelection chances at slightly better than 50% for most of this year, and now put the odds slightly against him:

The latest unwelcome news, before the country took a national holiday to welcome the pontiff, came from the US, where President Donald Trump has been a keen supporter of a kindred unconventional right-winger. Last year’s midterms in Argentina swung in Milei’s favor after US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a package of $20 billion to support Argentina’s bonds and currency — providing the president’s party won the elections. It brought yields down and delivered a surprise victory.

A Democratic Congress in Washington can probably thwart such aid next year — and that has sent Argentine bond yields sharply higher:

The deterioration in bonds this year has been matched by a further steady devaluation of the peso, which tends to drive up inflation:

That’s bad news, but doesn’t rob Milei of his chance of a second term. He just needs to succeed in his primary task of reining in inflation . It’s currently running at 34%, which counts as a success by Argentine standards. That’s lower than the rate at the end of 2019, when the previous right-wing reformist government under President Mauricio Macri was voted out of office, and it’s also below the rate Milei inherited from his Peronist predecessor, Alberto Fernandez. The nation has survived the shock that his austerity policies was always going to induce, prompting a rebuke from the pope on Sunday. Now, he must rein in price rises a little more:

As for his impact on investors, Milei has presided over a very un-Argentine boring period. In dollar terms, the benchmark Merval index is almost exactly where it was three years ago. That’s way behind the rest of the developing world, but impressive given the bitter medicine Milei has administered:

All depends on the economy over the next 12 months. My unscientific survey in the last few days suggests most would take the “over” — people think he can probably hold on. But they’re not as excited by Mr. Chainsaw as they are by Pope Leo, or by the extraordinary 95th-minute goal with which Buenos Aires team Boca Juniors snatched the league this weekend.

Another DeepSeek Moment

Shocking news from China can damage Nvidia Corp., the dominant semiconductor-maker. We learned that last year when word broke that DeepSeek had produced a competitive large language model. Nvidia far underperformed the main Nasdaq 100 that day — which turned out to be a buying opportunity for chip stocks:

Monday brought Nvidia’s worst performance since then, as it failed to join in the Nasdaq 100’s 3.4% rally . The news this time was that CEO Jensen Huang had had an affair with a suspected Chinese spy . The instant market reaction was that it was terrible news for Nvidia, but without wider repercussions:

Compared to the S&P 500 Equal-Weight index, Nvidia is remarkably lagging over the two years since the last presidential election. The fall before the allegations was already precipitous:

Nvidia’s market cap is now down more than $1.5 trillion in one month. It’s also stunningly cheap. On a blended forward 12-month basis, its price/earnings ratio is just above 10, its lowest since 2013:

For comparison, the S&P 500 trades at 17.7, and consumer staples at 23.6. If what was recently the hottest growth juggernaut on earth now trades at less than half the multiple of makers of diapers or toasters, it’s worth asking if this is overdone.

The allegations are obviously terrible for Huang himself . But it’s driven a huge reassessment of the prior certainty that Nvidia stood to be the clearest AI winner.

How can the honey-trap scandal on its own drive such a shift? Huang is a key man . He may well have to resign, and has no clear succession plan. Market pricing implies that Nvidia will be helpless without him. If that’s true, it shouldn’t be — no individual should be that important to an enterprise of Nvidia’s scale.

If this story has a broader impact, it concerns governance. The G in ESG (environmental, social and governance) investing has fallen out of favor as the entire concept has been subsumed into political culture wars. It was memorably dismissed by failed Ohio governorship candidate Vivek Ramaswamy as “Woke, Inc.” and he had a point. But whatever your views on environmentalism, worrying about corporate governance is not woke. There’s a really strong case for avoiding companies with problematic governance, such as requiring excessive trust in one, or a few, key individuals.

Massive IPOs are due in the next few months in which the founders hope to avoid surrendering much control. The potential merger of Tesla Inc. and SpaceX will involve huge trust in Elon Musk, possibly the world’s most controversial human . This issue has been ignored for too long.

Woke 1.0 went too far, as its practitioners now accept, and there are huge flaws in the way ESG has been applied. But Ramaswamy’s election defeat last week suggests the anti-woke backlash also went too far. It’s time to re-establish a disciplined focus on corporate governance.

Affordability and Expectations

Where does the affordability problem come from? Naturally prices are an important component, but so are incomes. Inflation at 3% would be much less of a problem if people expected their pay to go up by more. But they don’t.

The latest survey of consumer expectations by the New York Federal Reserve, largely conducted before last week’s sharp fall in oil prices, revealed expected inflation for the next 12 months of 3.9% — too high for the central bank’s comfort. But just as significant, particularly in explaining last week’s election results, is their expectation for wage increases of only 2.6%:

Outside of the post-pandemic bulge in prices, this is the greatest gap between the two since 2013. People seem to have given up on any hope that their pay can keep up with inflation. If there’s a key to the affordability crisis, it lies in changing that.

Emerging Disadvantage

Even as US markets rebounded, emerging markets are still reeling from fears that a Democratic sweep will lead to tighter oversight of artificial intelligence. As EM equities are dominated by the likes of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., Samsung Electronics Co. and SK Hynix Inc., the global selloff is understandable.

It’s not clear that US tech stocks’ recovery will drive a comparable rebound in emerging markets. Efforts by Taiwan’s market regulator to restrict short-selling show that idiosyncratic responses could jump-start investor confidence, as they coincided with Monday’s rally.

The broader EM index excluding China is up by about 22% year-to-date — but still has a long way to go before recapturing the heights attained in June. With AI regulation risks looming in Washington, the rebound could be hard to sustain. HSBC’s Alastair Pinder argues that the reaction to the midterms so far has been overdone. Even in a worst-case scenario, he argues, tighter oversight would slow AI capital expenditure rather than completely derail it. That should limit the damage to emerging markets.

However, Oxford Economics’ Sergi Lanau argues that EM’s growing tech-related exports are a major source of vulnerability. Exports reversed following the dot-com bust, and another sudden unwinding in tech stocks could trigger a similar reckoning. Lanau bases his doomsday scenario on analysis showing that the rise in AI-related imports dwarfs the boom a quarter-century ago, even after adjusting for differences in gross domestic product:

Any such bust would be sharp, judging by the 55% drop in US IT imports after the dot-com bubble, while the rise in AI-related trade has been rapid:

EM domestic value added embedded in US AI-related imports is rising, and the experience in 2001-2002 suggests that it could contract quickly. As in the dot-com bubble, permanent increases in domestic value added tied to the US AI industry appear feasible, but sharp short-term swings are unavoidable if the AI cycle turns.

Against this, the midterms have also brought hopes of a ceasefire in the US-Iran conflict and a sustained decline in oil prices. That would be very welcome, and Brent crude dropped a further 5.5% on Monday. But oil-importing emerging markets like India have been here before. Investors will need more assurances before venturing back.
