Oh Anthropic

Nvidia Corp.’s stock was up about 31% yesterday on news of Jensen Huang’s return as chief executive officer. The Nasdaq 100 Index was up almost 7%, and in general the artificial intelligence trade came roaring back after a tough week last week. Anthropic PBC, though, was down 1.4% yesterday, closing at $87.64, just 5.6% above the $83 share price of its initial public offering last week. It fell again this morning, breaking below the IPO price ; at noon it was trading at about $77.39.

It is early yet, but I wonder what Anthropic’s correlation with Jensen Huang is. On the one hand:

  1. Anthropic’s rise from nothing to a trillion-dollar valuation occurred at around the same time as Nvidia’s rise from about $320 billion to about $4 trillion yesterday. The same broad forces have been similarly good for Nvidia and Anthropic.
  2. There is a general broad artificial intelligence trade, in which investors bet on the speed and scale of the AI revolution, and Anthropic and Nvidia are big parts of it. When investors get enthusiastic about the AI trade, they buy Nvidia and Anthropic stock; when they get nervous, they sell. Nvidia and Anthropic are correlated in the minds and portfolios of investors.
  3. That AI trade depends heavily on raising trillions of dollars to finance AI infrastructure. Anthropic needs lots of that financing to pay for computing power, and Nvidia and Huang are crucial to that economy, as a provider of collateral and a coordinator of financing. That’s true in general — Nvidia is mobilizing hundreds of billions of dollars of chip financing, etc. — and also for Anthropic specifically: Anthropic has a “ strategic partnership with NVIDIA ,” and Nvidia was an anchor investor in Anthropic’s IPO.

On the other hand, there is a basic rivalry between Nvidia and frontier AI labs like Anthropic. AI will, the market assumes, create trillions of dollars of value, and the battle is over who will capture that value. One possibility is that intelligence will be a commodity and chips will be a monopoly: Lots of AI labs will sell interchangeably good models at low margins, but all of those models will run on Nvidia chips and pay Nvidia whatever it asks. Another possibility is that chips will be a commodity, or at least a competitive industry with multiple big firms and low margins, while one or two frontier AI labs like Anthropic will dominate intelligence and capture huge margins for themselves.

And so Anthropic is doing everything it can to encourage rivals to Nvidia in the chip business, while Nvidia is doing everything it can to encourage rivals to Anthropic in the models business. And when I say “Nvidia is doing everything it can,” I mean especially Huang. The Information reports :

One major factor in Trump and Vance’s involvement in Huang’s return to Nvidia, according to the person: Their view that the CEO was a critical figure in protecting open-source AI models and preventing closed-source model companies like Anthropic and OpenAI from becoming too powerful. Those companies “can’t have a monopoly and Jensen is one way to make sure it doesn’t happen,” one open-source AI executive said on Monday following Huang’s reinstatement.

“Nvidia’s Shocking U-Turn Is Bad News for Anthropic,” writes my Bloomberg Opinion colleague Parmy Olson, for similar reasons: Huang is a promoter of open-weight AI models that compete with Anthropic, and pretty blasé about Chinese competitors getting access to chips, while Anthropic “has pushed for stricter export bans, to stop Chinese AI easily catching up to Silicon Valley’s frontier models” and has been skeptical of open-weight models.

One possibility is that, from about 2021 to about 2026, “the AI trade” was basically one trade , and what was good for AI was good for Anthropic and Nvidia and the rest of the industry. But as the AI trade increasingly becomes the entire economy, perhaps the dispersion will increasingly happen within the AI industry. “Open-weight stocks were up today as frontier-lab stocks were down,” that sort of thing.

It would be funny and satisfying if I could tell a story like “AI stocks went up yesterday because Jensen Huang came back and he’s good for AI, but Anthropic’s stock went down yesterday because Huang is bad for Anthropic specifically .” But I don’t really think that’s true. My guess is that Anthropic was heading down anyway yesterday, and Huang’s return actually kept Anthropic’s stock higher than it would otherwise have been. My guess is that, at least yesterday, the Huang-led AI relief rally was good for Anthropic. Anthropic and Nvidia are both down today.

My guess is that Anthropic was heading down because it has been heading down more or less since it started trading. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share last Wednesday. It opened for trading at $111.50 per share at 11:48 a.m. on Thursday, a 34% IPO pop, but quickly traded down; it was below $100 by 12:45 on Thursday, closed that day at $97.17, and finished the week at $88.91.

And it traded below the IPO price today, on Anthropic’s fourth day as a public company. This is traditionally considered a bad thing, and the traditional view is that it is the job of the IPO underwriters to prevent it. Some ways to prevent it are:

  • Do a good job of marketing the stock, so lots of investors want to buy it in the IPO and hold it, or add to their positions, afterward;
  • Make early investors and employees sign lockup agreements so that a lot of stock doesn’t flood into the market right after the IPO; and
  • Using the greenshoe to stabilize the stock once it starts trading.

The marketing seems to have been basically successful. Anthropic got something like $500 billion of orders for its $100 billion IPO, and, as we discussed on Thursday , most of the investors who put in orders in the IPO got zero shares, creating immediate demand for the stock when it opened. The investors who got zeroed felt like they had to own Anthropic, so they put in orders to buy at the open on Thursday, creating a lot of demand against relatively little supply.

But that changed quickly. The people who bought at the open had the shares they needed. But other people were free to sell. The Wall Street Journal reported :

Some investors question how the company and its bankers doled out the $81 billion worth of shares in the IPO. More than half went to investors who already had stakes in the company, including Altimeter Capital, Lightspeed Venture Partners and Menlo Ventures, according to people familiar with the matter, an unusually high portion reflecting Anthropic executives’ desire to reward early backers.

Some of those early backers were venture-capital firms, which were prevented from selling their pre-existing stakes. Those firms don’t typically get big allotments in IPO shares, which they would be free to sell immediately to lock in gains. ...

Anthropic’s IPO also had a unique provision that allowed some current and former employees to immediately sell around 300 million shares, or around $26 billion worth at the IPO price. Typically, employees are subject to lockups that prevent them from selling for several months. Sales of those shares likely added downward pressure on the company’s stock.

A plausible story might be that the opening auction had a lot of price-insensitive buyers who really needed to own the stock, while the later trading had a lot of price-sensitive sellers (employees, VCs with allocations) who were happy to cash out. Anthropic got a big immediate pop, but it fell from there.

The way the greenshoe, or “overallotment option,” works is that, in the IPO, the banks sell 115% as many shares as the company is actually offering, and the company gives them a (free) option to buy the extra 15% at the IPO price. So Anthropic offered 980 million shares in its IPO, which means that, when the deal priced:

  1. Anthropic sold its banks 980 million shares, and
  2. Its banks sold 1,127 million shares to investors.

The banks were short 147 million shares. The theory is that, if the stock traded below the IPO price, the banks would have “ammunition” to “support” it: That just means that they could buy back those 147 million shares in the market, at a profit for themselves , to keep the price from going down too much. On the other hand, if the stock traded above the IPO price, the banks were not at risk on their shorts: They wouldn’t have to buy back the stock at market prices (at a loss to themselves), but could exercise the greenshoe option to buy back the extra 147 million shares at the $83 IPO price.

That would come in handy now, but oops, the banks already exercised the greenshoe option — and bought back their 147 million-share short position from Anthropic at $83 per share — last week, when the stock was trading above the IPO price. The banks are no longer short, so they can’t buy today to support the price. At the Financial Times, Craig Coben asks “whether the underwriters of the IPO were overly hasty in exercising the full overallotment option or ‘greenshoe.’” “Why then didn’t Anthropic’s underwriters wait a few days just to make sure the stock settled into a stable pattern?”

The greenshoe has two slightly conflicting purposes. On the one hand, there is the classic explanation I laid out above, giving the banks ammunition to support the stock price when it starts trading. This is the explanation favored by capital markets bankers and investors.

On the other hand, the greenshoe is more money for the company: When Anthropic’s banks exercised the greenshoe, they paid Anthropic an extra $12 billion for those 147 million extra shares. Anthropic could use an extra $12 billion. It would rather have that money than not; it would rather have a bigger IPO than a smaller one. When I was an equity capital markets banker, more than one client said something to the effect of “we’d really prefer it if you’d promise to exercise the greenshoe.” No! The point of the greenshoe, from the banks’ perspective, is that it is not guaranteed to be exercised; it allows the banks the flexibility to buy stock and support the price. [1] But the company might not care that much, and from the company’s perspective, the point of the greenshoe is to get more money.

SpaceXsla

For that matter, what is the correlation between Tesla Inc. and SpaceX? That is more empirically knowable, but what should it be? Tesla’s stock closed yesterday at $385.46 per share. SpaceX’s closed at $133.35 per share. One Tesla share is worth about as much as 2.9 SpaceX shares; that ratio has ranged from about 2.0 to about 3.2 since SpaceX went public this summer. We all more or less know that SpaceX is going to acquire Tesla, one of these days, in an all-stock merger. How much should it pay?

I feel like there is a narrow range of reasonable answers. If SpaceX paid 2.9 shares for every Tesla share — a 0% premium to current trading prices, traditional in a “merger of equals” — that seems fine. If SpaceX paid 3.2 shares for every Tesla share — a 10% premium — that would also be reasonable: Tesla shareholders would get a little extra value to induce them to exchange into the much more unilaterally Musk-controlled SpaceX. I suppose you could take the average ratio over the last month or whatever and get a slightly lower number; that would be more or less justified. Basically the reasonable range is like 2.8 to 3.2. That’s not that big a range as a relative matter, though it does represent a difference of about $200 billion to Tesla shareholders.

There are weird dynamics here, though. Imagine I told you: “SpaceX and Tesla will announce a merger in the next two months, at a 0% premium to the average ratio over the 10 trading days leading up to the announcement.” On the one hand, in some business sense there’d be no reason to buy SpaceX instead of Tesla or Tesla instead of SpaceX: Whichever stock you buy will just turn into stock of combined SpaceX/Tesla, so there’s no reason to favor one over the other. If Tesla announces good earnings, that’s good for SpaceX, etc. These companies are in a sense pre-merged, so they should trade together.

On the other hand, the actual merger ratio matters, and has not yet been determined. If Tesla announces good earnings and its stock goes up more than SpaceX’s, then Tesla investors will get more of the combined company. If Tesla announces good earnings, and you’re a Tesla shareholder, maybe you should buy as much Tesla stock as possible to push up the exchange ratio. Though you can’t be sure that your buying will actually influence the exchange ratio.

Or vice versa. Elon Musk owns about 46% of SpaceX and about 20% of Tesla. A merger will, in effect, dilute his economic ownership of SpaceX: He’ll be handing out shares to more outside investors in Tesla. (Presumably a merger will not do much to change his control of SpaceX, which is absolute .) If Tesla’s stock were to go down , then SpaceX could in theory pay fewer shares for it, so he would suffer less dilution (and get a greater share of Tesla). It might be optimal for Musk personally if he spent the next few months:

  • saying good things about SpaceX’s business and prospects,
  • saying bad things about Tesla’s business and prospects,
  • doing lucrative cool new AI projects at SpaceX, and
  • doing ugly expensive new projects at Tesla.

Everything will end up in the same place, but the relative valuation will determine how much he owns of the combination. And he has a certain amount of control over that relative valuation.

In practice, Tesla has mostly gone up against SpaceX over the past couple of months, and my sense is that Elon Musk does not think much about this stuff and has bigger fish to fry. Nonetheless. When the merger is announced, whatever the exchange ratio is, there will surely be a lawsuit arguing that he sandbagged Tesla’s value to increase his own stake.

Anyway, here’s a Financial Times story about the special committees that are negotiating the Tesla/SpaceX merger in the very long shadow of Musk

The two sides must then hammer out a fair “exchange ratio” for SpaceX and Tesla shares. This will be closely watched by institutional investors as well as Musk’s army of retail shareholders, who hold an estimated 20 per cent of the electric vehicle maker and are very bullish on the company’s valuation.

“There is more friction than people assume. I don’t think the committees are close yet,” one of the people said. “But the culture of Elon’s companies is to work 24 hours a day, seven days a week and then execute immediately.” ...

If the financial terms can be settled, the companies will hash out governance rights and who takes crucial roles in the merged entity, including whether there is a chair, who will be chief financial officer and which directors will remain. “Elon will decide that and decide it quickly,” said one person. “No one is under any illusions: this is his company.”

Honestly none of this seems that hard to me. The ratio is just, you know, pick a day, see what the market ratio is, and use that; people will complain about the same amount no matter what ratio you pick. (Probably err in favor of Tesla, just because Tesla’s shareholders are more likely, and more able, to complain than SpaceX’s.) The “governance rights,” come on! “Who takes crucial roles in the merged entity”? I bet I know who!

Stablecoin debanking

One theory of crypto is that you can’t trust banks. If you put money in a bank, and the US government decides it doesn’t like you, it will make the bank freeze your money. Or even if the bank decides that it doesn’t like you, it might “debank” you, not quite taking your money but inconveniencing you and perhaps cutting you out of the financial system. This all can seem shadowy and arbitrary, and part of the point of crypto was to replace the discretionary power of banks with impartial code. If you have the private key to a crypto wallet, nobody can take your crypto away from you.

But that certainly isn’t true of stablecoins. A stablecoin is a cryptocurrency — transferrable on the blockchain, not reliant on any trusted intermediary, etc. — that represents a claim for $1 from the balance sheet of a central intermediary like Circle or Tether. Nobody can take your stablecoins away from you. But Circle or Tether could always declare that your stablecoins are invalid: They could say “these stablecoins no longer represent a $1 claim on our balance sheet; these stablecoins are now worthless.” In fact, they can go further and tell their smart contracts to block transfers of your stablecoins. As a matter of narrow crypto theory, nobody can take your stablecoins away from you. As a matter of economic practice, Circle and Tether sure can.

We talked about this a few weeks ago, when Circle and Tether quickly froze hundreds of millions of dollars of stablecoins stolen from Binance by hackers and gave the money back to Binance. Because that’s absolutely a thing they can do.

As a general matter, would you expect Circle and Tether to exercise this power more arbitrarily than traditional banks, or less arbitrarily? More frequently, or less frequently? With more legal process, or less legal process? Bloomberg’s Ryan Weeks and Olga Kharif report :

As North Korean cyberthieves were funneling billions out of Binance last month, the world’s largest crypto exchange looked to two other companies to help stop the bleeding: Tether and Circle.

The duo, which issue cryptocurrencies that mimic the US dollar, reacted unusually quickly to freeze accounts hackers were using to move their haul around. It was a sign of the seriousness of the hack, the industry’s maturation, and the most public showcasing yet of the near-total control Tether and Circle exert over the tokens they created. They dominate the market, with their two main stablecoins together representing 86% of all in circulation. …

Tether has been criticized for not having a consistent modus operandi, sometimes allowing tokens to slide away unimpeded and other times seizing them indefinitely. It sometimes charges a fee to eventually return them, according to customers who described their experiences. Meanwhile, Circle’s philosophy until recently had been to only act after receiving a court order, a method so structured and time-consuming that the judge’s decision could be irrelevant by the time it came.

But during the Binance cybertheft, both companies acted almost immediately — in a fashion Circle referred to as “machine speed.” Luckily for Binance, which owns a stake in Circle, the company took a different tack than in some prior hacking episodes. … The speed of the Binance response stands in contrast to the experience of victims of earlier hacks, some of whom have spent years trying to recover frozen funds.

Well, right, Binance is a big customer, so the stablecoin issuers acted quickly to get its money back.

Oh Jensen

I joked yesterday , and on the Money Stuff podcast last week, that Jensen Huang’s actions left Nvidia Corp.’s board of directors with no choice but to get rid of him, but that once the board fulfilled that obligation there was no obstacle to bringing him back:

There is a certain technicality to all of this. There seems to be a view that certain actions — having an affair with an alleged spy, having an affair with a subordinate, having another affair with another subordinate — are disqualifying, in the sense that, if a CEO does them, the board has no choice but to remove him. But these bright-line rules do not give any guidance for what happens next. “If your CEO does X, you have to fire him,” fine, but then what? Then you have to hire a new CEO. Whom do you hire? The most qualified candidate. What if it’s the old CEO? Apparently that’s fine! Misconduct in his previous job (as CEO of Nvidia) is not disqualifying for his next job (as CEO of Nvidia). The board discharged its obligation by getting rid of Huang, so now it is free to bring him back.

I thought this was a correct analysis of the situation facing the board, but also stupid. This is not a good rule. “If the CEO does X, you have to fire him” suggests that X is actually disqualifying; a merely technical rule like “you have to demonstrate zero tolerance for X by firing your CEO for doing it, but you don’t actually care about X so you can bring him right back” is lawless and also pointless. If you don’t care about X, don’t fire him; if you do care, care for real.

I missed that US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent actually endorsed this theory . A week ago, he went on television to say that Nvidia under Huang had a “ culture of noncompliance ,” that “they need a dramatic shake-up ,” and that “they need to go outside the company” to find a new CEO. But after President Donald Trump decided to bring Huang back, Bessent pivoted :

On Sunday, Bessent was asked on CBS’s “Face the Nation” about his previous comments that Nvidia’s board should consider someone from outside the company to succeed Huang. “Jensen is on the outside now … I said that they should go to the outside, but he’s on the outside now.”

Incredible work. “They need a dramatic shakeup to change their whole culture, and the best way to get that is by bringing in a fresh outsider like Jensen Huang.” Everything is so transparently dumb and cynical.

Space data centers

Every so often, young people in college or law school or business school ask me what they should do with their lives, what legal or financial specialties are most likely to be stable and lucrative over the next few decades. Of course I have no idea. A decent general heuristic is that you should try to become an expert in some field that (1) is going to be big but (2) nobody knows much about now, because you will be able to become a leading expert much more quickly and with less competition. A lot of 22-year-olds got very rich in the crypto boom, because they knew as much about crypto as anyone else, because nobody knew anything about crypto when they started.

Similarly, if you are a law student today, maybe you should get into space data center law? (This is not career advice and is like 75% a joke.) It seems fair to say that nobody knows anything about space data center law, because there are no space data centers and space law in general is not a big practice area at big law firms. But maybe there will be space data centers, and if there are, somebody is going to have to go around perfecting security interests in the chips inside of them, and why shouldn’t that be you?

This is already becoming a thing, as we discussed yesterday : Lenders in SpaceX’s big recent chip financing package have pushed to prevent SpaceX from shooting the chips into space, because if it did that and then defaulted, the lenders would have trouble seizing (or finding) the collateral. The lenders’ lawyers are already practicing space data center law, though in a purely negative way. Think how useful you could be if you were able to say “no, it’s fine, we’ll use the Space Commercial Code to secure control of our collateral in space,” or whatever. Don’t come to me with space data center problems; come to me with space data center solutions.

There are some obvious downsides to this practice area. For one thing, the travel could be brutal. If you have to go do due diligence on data centers in the asteroid belt, you’ll be away from your family for years with terrible room service. Also it seems likely that Elon Musk will be either your client or your counterparty in any space data center deal, which seems stressful. But here’s a recent paper on space data center law by … space lawyer … Alex Li:

Orbital data centers (“ODCs”) place computing infrastructure out of Earth’s atmosphere and beyond physical reach. Companies have already tested artificial intelligence processing in Outer Space and proposed mega-constellations dedicated to orbital computing. But thus far, the legal debates around such infrastructure have focused on data protection issues. This Article addresses a more fundamental question: who can actually control a ODC if something goes wrong? Existing space laws grant “jurisdiction and control” over a space object to its State of registry and require States to authorize and provide continuing supervision over private space activities. When it comes to conventional satellites, legal responsibility and practical control have largely gone hand-in-hand. But an ODC can pull them apart. A command might depend on foreign infrastructure, third-party credentials, or an operator that can go bankrupt. So a registering State may retain jurisdiction even as its practical control drifts away. This Article argues that States must be able to practically control an ODC in order to effectively supervise it.

We’ll need new rules, new contracts, new financing structures, new everything for space data centers; you can get in now on the ground floor.
