After a Blue Tuesday, No Black Wednesday

Markets and voters like springing surprises. Last week, Brazil’s voters upended expectations by returning Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva to power; markets freaked out. Now in the US, Democrats have swept the Congress and state offices in a conclusive fashion that nobody had dared predict — and markets responded with relief.

This ends a period when President Donald Trump could govern as though Congress didn’t exist, and opens an array of possibilities for the 2028 election. The initial expectation was that a Democratic landslide would be market-negative. That’s how US stock futures treated the ever more implausible Democratic gains in the Senate — until turning around at midnight on the East Coast:

This seems bizarre but there’s precedent for it, ironically on the night when Trump shocked the world by winning the presidency in 2016. During the campaign, the stock market had repeatedly sold off on any good news for Trump. That was detailed in a paper for the Brookings Institution , which extrapolated these selloffs to predict that an election win would bring the S&P 500 down 10-15%.

As election night unfolded, stock futures fell across the world. Then the result was confirmed, Trump gave a magnanimous acceptance speech, and markets turned around. The S&P 500 rallied 2.22% on the Monday before the election on polls showing a clear victory for Hillary Clinton; it surged a further 1.1% the Wednesday afterward once Trump had won.

How to explain this? When confronted with a fact, we can all begin to see some of the silver lining in outcomes we previously found alarming. Ten years ago, it took the Trump victory to confront traders with the fact that many of his policies could be good for growth. Something similar is afoot this time. Here are some key points:

  • It’s a personal defeat for Trump; not necessarily for Trumpism

This is a massive personal repudiation of Trump the man . Direct rejections of his handpicked candidates, like Ken Paxton in Texas, are particularly ringing rebukes. His political judgment appears finally to have deserted him, and his personal control over the Republican Party must at least be compromised.

But it’s dangerous to spin a narrative of defeat for right-wing populist politics . Anger against the man is so acute because he blatantly broke promises to fight inflation and avoid wars. This result doesn’t mean that there’s no chance of success for an honest right-wing populist who does what Trump said he was going to do. And given that some very moderate Democrats will now take power, it’s not a big endorsement for left-wing populism, either. As Trump’s erratic behavior had driven volatility, few investors will be perturbed to see his wings clipped. The result is more market-friendly than it at first appears.

  • Gridlock can be good for you

The slim chance that a surprise Republican majority would pass another tax cut has been extinguished. Amid the current fiscal anxiety, that’s probably good news. The presidential veto ensures that Congress can’t push through any massive fiscal expansion. Congressional investigations might slow the works of government, which would also be no bad thing for investors.

  • Inflation really, really matters

Price rises have now inflicted defeats on two presidents in a row. Surely there won’t be a third. Whoever next moves into the White House will accept that inflation constrains their actions. Policies of any political stripe that risk reigniting inflation are much less likely. The immediate move in inflation swaps suggests hawks like this result:

  • The Fed’s independence is back off the agenda

The Senate must approve new Fed appointments. Any attempt to pack the central bank with governors who will do the presidential bidding is now at an end. With the resumption of attempts to prosecute Fed Governor Lisa Cook (who voted last month to hike rates ), this did not go without saying. There are many problems with the Fed, but attempting to overhaul it this way was always a bad idea, and one more market risk has been removed.

  • The feared near-term uncertainty isn’t going to happen

The scale of the Democratic victory brooks no argument. Protracted and consequential lawsuits in states like Alaska won’t happen; there will be no new Jan. 6. One big risk has been allayed.

Some negatives remain:

  • 2028 is suddenly much more uncertain

The Democrats did a great job of finding good candidates for the specific circumstances of states like Kansas, Texas or South Carolina. It will be far harder to agree on one candidate for the entire country in 2028, and the decisive nature of the victory will probably encourage more to enter. The risk of a fire-breathing egalitarian whom markets would really hate (like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez) must be taken ever more seriously.

For the Republicans, the assumption had been that whoever Trump endorsed would win the nomination. As a lame duck deemed culpable for this loss, it’s no longer clear he has that power. That’s good for the functioning of US democracy, and for Republicans’ long-term future; for the next two years, it likely means a mess, with several viable candidates covering a range from isolationism to classic business-friendly Republicans.

  • Lame ducks can be dangerous

Trump cannot run again, but he retains the veto, and a range of executive powers that he’s shown no compunction in using. Foreign Affairs points out that a lame duck can still start a war, while European diplomats have briefed that he could be “more mad dog than lame duck.” Much rests on the psychology of one notoriously mercurial man who now has little left to lose.

  • AI’s politicization just ratcheted up

Data centers’ huge unpopularity had much to do with the Democrat Gina Hinojosa’s capture of the Texas governorship. She and many city mayors have the power to limit data-center growth and must now follow through. This risk to the AI trade just got bigger — it’s not clear that Democrats can force any broader “AI pause” or impose greater regulation, because of the presidential veto, but that issue is now very much live for 2028.

Endowments...

For decades, the Yale Model, pioneered by David Swensen at Yale University’s endowment, set the gold standard for long-term investing. Applying efficient-market theory, he took advantage of Yale’s infinite time horizon to pick out a range of long-term illiquid investments in inefficient markets, working out correctly that this would give him the best chance to deliver returns above the public markets.

For Ivies, the returns have helped deal with the financial pressures that the Trump administration has imposed — but the reliance on illiquid alternative assets restricts their room for maneuver when they need access to funds, as happened blatantly in 2008 when Swensen and others needed to borrow to meet their cash commitments.

The approach can still deliver big time, as shown by Washington University in St. Louis, which posted a return of more than 37% for fiscal year 2026, beating the average Ivy League endowment by more than 15 percentage points. But can such gains be sustainable, and what risks have managers taken?

Using annual data to construct factor-mimicking portfolios with a methodology detailed here , Michael Markov of Markov Processes International finds that WashU has some of the highest volatility among endowments, thanks in particular to concentrated co-investments in private companies, including AI-related investments.

That concentration, Markov argues, reflects a broader issue for all endowments. How much risk to take, where to invest, and whether to hedge exposures can all drive returns more than selecting the underlying funds. Some endowments, he says, are making consequential investment decisions that aren’t apparent to outside observers:

Taking more risk, hedging, these are indications that basically endowments are being run like a trading desk… Do we know that boards know about risk-taking? Do they know about the hedging decisions and things? How much do they know?

None of this shows that WashU has taken on more risk than its peers. The pertinent question is whether university boards fully understand their exposures and how they fit with their financial needs. Markov says the approach was rewarded last year — and that offers an opportune time for institutions to reconsider how much risk they want to carry.

This exercise requires assessing how the portfolio might fare in a severe downturn, when the university still has to meet spending commitments and fund private-market capital call:

The key of the endowment model is not leverage and not concentration. Swensen’s idea was diversification. That’s the one thing. You take as many risk factors as much, hopefully not correlated. Hopefully, it comes from different things. You have energy, real estate, you have private, public, emerging markets, and these different things that should behave differently in different environments.

Investments in standout companies such as SpaceX can pay off handsomely, but they’re high-risk, long-term bets. Not making them is not necessarily a mistake; but taking a bet and winning is not on its own a sound investment strategy.
