Water on Troubled Oil

The oil market staged a dramatic reversal on Monday. Brent crude tumbled by 11.4%, its biggest fall in six months. Two questions arise. First, what news could have driven such a fall? And second, perhaps more important: How could oil possibly have been so expensive in the first place?

Crude prices have fluctuated with news from the Persian Gulf ever since the US and Israel attacked Iran in late February. Markets have since shown a willingness to take good news at face value. The biggest fall this year came when President Donald Trump trailed an “important update” on Iran. Oil fell almost as much as Monday back on March 10, when Trump said the war would “end soon.” It didn’t. So what was different about the latest news?

One difference this time, arguably, is that it’s Iran, not Trump, talking up peace. The money quote, from Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian, is:

We are striving to resolve the issue through dialogue and, in this regard, the Supreme Leader has also granted certain powers to the government.

There are no specifics, and no reason to take it more seriously than Trump’s promise eight months ago to end the war soon. But invoking Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei is meant to show that Iran means business.

Andrew Bishop of Signum Global Advisors argues that a return to diplomacy is more likely than the feared re-escalation:

  • The US has succeeded in creating a real threat of re-escalation that Iran could no longer ignore.
  • Despite its hawkish rhetoric, Iran would prefer to make modest concessions than to be attacked once again.
  • Mediators and other third parties (incl. China) are leaning heavily on Iran to compromise.
  • The Trump administration – despite its own hawkish rhetoric – would prefer to forestall a return to war.

Monday’s selloff could thus be a measure of the administration’s success in convincing everyone that it was prepared to re-escalate. That’s what the market was discounting, or such a big drop would not have been possible.

Official data on supply coming from the Gulf suggests that oil is indeed beginning to flow again, despite the ongoing disruption to the Strait of Hormuz:

These numbers need a health warning. Peter Tchir of Academy Securities points out that Iranian oil has “slowed to a trickle” with the US blockade, while increased shipping times and costs have elevated prices for refineries around the globe:

Finally, and more importantly, diesel, LNG, and fertilizer are not being exported at even half of the prewar levels (by even optimistic estimates).

The point remains that the situation is not as bad as when crude prices peaked in April. But that is not what Brent has been suggesting. The front-month future almost made a new high, while the contract for delivery at the end of next year, now at $87, had never been higher:

It’s hard to explain this unless traders expected an intensification and prolongation of the war. That doesn’t just show up in the oil price itself. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution argues that fear of war is driving a range of asset prices historically correlated with oil:

The S&P 500, French debt, gold and silver are getting hit as anxiety over a return to a “hot” war builds. These moves are entirely in line with these assets’ historical correlation with oil, which means current losses should reverse quickly once we make it past this period of maximum uncertainty.

This might seem strange, as Iran and its impact on inflation has been a key issue in Tuesday’s midterm elections that are expected to give Trump a bloody nose. But the risk of war is relatively impervious to US domestic politics. Will Denyer of Gavekal Research points to 2006, when Republicans lost both chambers thanks to the unpopular Iraq war:

Despite this, the Republican President George W. Bush was able to announce a new “surge” of troops into Iraq in January 2007, using existing military appropriations. After that, the Democrats tried to pass funding legislation with troop withdrawal conditions, but the president vetoed it...

That episode shows that even when a war is deeply unpopular and the Democrats win a decisive victory in Congress, it does not guarantee a swift end to hostilities.

Much depends on reading Trump’s mind, which is never easy. It’s in his interests to get out of the war, and it looks like his bellicosity could force Iran to negotiate . But a tendency to ignore Congress is only likely to intensify if his friends are no longer in charge of it. As an “old man in a hurry” focused on his legacy, he may well do what he believes it takes to avoid being saddled with the stigma of a lost war — and the midterms won’t affect that.

One corollary: If escalation comes to pass, stocks would take much more of a battering. A second: If Trump and Khamenei really do find some durable compromise, there’s still a lot of war premium in oil that would then unwind. It’s infuriating that an election being driven by precisely this issue seems almost irrelevant, but those are the stakes.

Grand Theft Crypto

Cryptography, which underpins cryptocurrencies, is designed to make transactions difficult to compromise. But last week’s Binance heist, along with a host of other smaller thefts before it, shows that advances in technology such as artificial intelligence can push the boundaries of what’s possible, especially for well-resourced criminals looking to exploit weaknesses in crypto systems.

What was new about the Binance heist was its sheer scale:

While a full technical post-mortem of how hackers stole more than $6.3 billion has yet to emerge, the thieves, reportedly backed by the North Korean state, may have used AI at some stage. As Binance tries to recover the loot, much is at stake. The point of Bitcoin, arguably, is that transactions are irreversible (as Bloomberg Opinion’s Matt Levine points out) . That becomes a critical disadvantage compared to a conventional bank once security is breached. Scarce wonder the news prompted traders to pull at least $4.7 billion in net withdrawals from Binance as of Monday.

With outflows representing less than 3% of the $166 billion in assets held by the world’s largest crypto exchange, it is worth asking whether they could develop into a broader run on the platform and whether it could withstand a more sustained withdrawal of funds.

One silver lining is that preliminary findings point toward compromised internal systems and authorization controls rather than a fundamental vulnerability in blockchain technology. Arca’s Jeff Dorman argues that the reputational damage may be far harder to repair. As the industry’s largest exchange, Binance is a major source of liquidity and a critical intermediary for traders, institutions, and token issuers worldwide. To quote Dorman, the company is “effectively the JPMorgan of crypto.”

Yet unlike JPMorgan, Binance does not have a traditional banking charter, FDIC insurance, or an established government safety net behind it. Its business depends heavily on customers trusting that their assets are secure. If an institution of Binance’s size and sophistication can be compromised so spectacularly, what does that say about the hundreds of smaller exchanges and custodians operating throughout the industry?

For the broader crypto world, this is the latest spoke in the wheels of an industry craving institutional acceptance. It is yet another stark reminder that, for all cryptocurrencies’ security features, crypto firms still haven’t mastered the basic task of safeguarding customers’ money. To add salt to the wound — the hit to credibility — Thursday’s incident came at a time when Bitcoin and Ether had appeared to be stabilizing after a brutal run:

For now, the fallout appears contained, although a return to pre-hack levels will require, at the very least, clarity on the steps being taken to prevent a recurrence. That reassurance may prove difficult. Regarding the broader implications for crypto, Cantor’s Gareth Gacetta argues that further analysis of the hack’s root causes and customer flows is necessary before drawing wider conclusions. Still, he believes the industry can emerge stronger:

While rising concerns around AI-assisted hacks could weigh on fund flows near term, we continue to believe crypto is a beneficiary of agentic finance and that incidents such as this ultimately strengthen smart-contract resilience. On its own, we do not view this event as sufficient to derail what we continue to see as the early innings of a broader crypto bull market cycle.

More than a year after Bitcoin peaked and began to fall, US spot funds have shed roughly $2.6 billion net, though flows have turned more positive recently, with about $4.8 billion entering since July. Ultimately, Gacetta argues that Binance’s response demonstrates an ecosystem capable of containing a major breach rather than one that poses a source of long-term systemic risk. (Others such as Bloomberg colleague Lionel Laurent are skeptical of this.)

Gacetta remains constructive. He points to growing involvement by regulated US venues or exchanges, while banks, custodians, and technology providers move to blockchain despite the recent price weakness. But the Binance heist suggests that an asset class that was founded with the aim of obviating any need for regulation is now in need of just such help.
