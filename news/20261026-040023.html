Whither the Trump Trade

The greatest waves are forced by the confluence of contradictory factors. At the Cortes Bank , 100 miles off California, an underwater island beneath where tides meet combines to create massive pillars of surf for adrenaline junkies to ride. Similar factors create monster waves off Tahiti, or Nazare in Portugal.

In markets, contradictory factors also create the greatest waves — and unlike ocean waves, the adrenaline addicts surfing them can affect how high they rise. As big-wave pioneer Buzzy Trent once said: “Waves are not measured in feet and inches, they are measured in increments of fear.”

At present, we have the separate but related bullish trade in artificial intelligence , now suffering an intense bout of skepticism, and a bearish trend in bonds. The two affect each other, with booming AI heightening the case for interest rates to rise, while prohibitive long-term rates prompt doubts about AI groups’ access to financing.

The result, viewed by the performance of the exchange-traded funds tracking the Nasdaq 100 and Treasuries of 20 years or longer, has been a spectacular wave that crested in the last month. This isn’t like the self-inflicted selloffs of Trump 2.0 after last year’s Liberation Day tariffs, and then the attack on Iran. But for now, the grand trend of rising tech stocks and falling bonds is intact:

Can this continue? Much depends on the financials of the companies at the center of AI . Results from Alphabet, Amazon.com and Microsoft this week could restore confidence, as could a successful IPO for Anthropic PBC, makers of Claude, next month. Bad outcomes would have the opposite effect.

Beneath this wave, oil is the undertow . Next month, once US and Israeli elections are over, hostilities in the Gulf could re-escalate. That would raise the risk of permanent damage to production facilities, and thus an ongoing hit to supply, and that is showing up in renewed bets that higher oil prices are going to be around for a long time.

This chart shows the rolling contract for the price of Brent 12 months forward, which hasn’t been this high since the immediate aftermath of Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, and the contract for November 2027, which has never been this high. Optimism that the war and the interruption to supply that it causes will soon be over has drained away:

If oil keeps rising like this, it will attack the presumptions of growth, and possibly accelerate the re-found tendency to use Treasuries as a haven in times of peril — and bring the AI/bonds wave crashing down. It will also create a dilemma for the Federal Reserve...

The Uses and Abuses of Purdah

The UK’s budget is due on Wednesday . It used to come shrouded in “Purdah” — the traditional Indian word for secluding women behind veils. Nobody on the chancellor’s team was supposed to breathe a word to anyone in the weeks before it was unveiled to the House of Commons.

Like Purdah itself, that concept is taken less seriously now, but the Fed adheres to its own version. Leading US central bank officials go silent in public for the last week and a half before each Federal Open Market Committee meeting. Under the new regime of Kevin Warsh, who is keen to ditch “forward guidance” to markets about the Fed’s intentions, that shouldn’t make much difference. But the period from the last meeting until the Fed’s Purdah started 10 days ago was nothing like that. This is how the chance of an October rate hike moved over that period, as gauged by the Bloomberg World Interest Rate Probability function:

There is a repeating pattern. New data, notably September consumer price inflation that showed a disquieting rise across the board, prompted traders to mark up the odds on a hike. Then a speech by a Fedster would bring those odds right back down again.

John Williams of the New York Fed talked down the sense that there was urgency to hike soon. Warsh himself suggested that he was in no hurry in a speech to the International Monetary Fund on the last day before Purdah. Politics add to the reasons to hold, as the Fed will want to avoid controversy a week before the midterms.

It looks like Warsh has already joined the ranks of Fed chairmen swiftly forced into retreat by markets once they take over. Instead of forward guidance, championed most famously by Ben Bernanke , he favors a kind of random walk in response to data. After alarmed market reactions to his first press conferences , it looks mightily as though he’s given up on this.

Meanwhile, something is moving rate expectations beyond Fedspeak or the data: the bond market. Rising long yields are a problem, and are perceived to force the central bank to demonstrate its bona fides by clamping down in the present. Of late, the 10-year yield and the market forecast for fed funds a year hence, have moved in alignment:

But what is the direction of causation? Gavekal’s Anatole Kaletsky suggests that the Fed is driving the bond market, for two reasons:

The first is that many bond investors are so confident about the Fed’s ability and willingness to hit its inflation target that they dismiss short-term fluctuations in data on inflation, fiscal policy, GDP and so on as irrelevant noise... A second reason... is the changing structure of the bond market. Bond trading today is dominated by hedge funds, algos and other short-term trend-followers for whom forecasts of inflation, or GDP or fiscal conditions 10 years ahead, or even one year ahead, are entirely irrelevant except insofar as they may influence the Fed’s decisions on short-term policy rates.

If Kaletsky is right, and rising yields continue to alarm the administration , then the pressure on Warsh to guide the market, whether veiled by Purdah or not, will only intensify.

Some sectors, as Apollo’s Torsten Slok has pointed out , have missed out completely from the benefits of artificial intelligence. Biotechnology is not one of them. Investors’ bet that AI will boost productivity is especially evident in drug discovery, where the technology is accelerating research and lowering development costs. The offshoots for biotech stocks, though not completely immune from the broader AI market selloff of recent weeks, are encouraging:

Also, look at dealmaking. In a market increasingly hostile to deals beyond mega-cap tech, five transactions this month reiterated investors’ appetite for biotech. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Jennie Li says this reflects a broader search to combine defensive earnings with innovation-driven growth. Decades ago, healthcare’s investment case rested largely on blockbuster drugs and stable cash flows:

Today, GLP-1 therapies, gene editing, AI-powered drug discovery, smarter clinical trials, antibody platforms, radiopharmaceuticals, advanced diagnostics, robotic surgery, and precision medicine are reshaping that case.

These have made healthcare a big AI beneficiary, and biotech deals show excitement about its growth potential:

Healthcare stocks are up about 5% over the past month, even as the AI tech stocks sold off. This resilience has rewarded investors in companies like Eli Lilly, reinforcing the appeal of businesses that pair resilient earnings with innovation in areas such as obesity, immunology and oncology:

Meanwhile, Washington is pushing to restrict US investment in Chinese biotech. The scrutiny by the Treasury is rooted in the Comprehensive Outbound Investment National Security Act, enacted last year, which provides a basis for restricting US investments in countries of concern — most importantly China.

Both administrative action and legislative pressure lie behind the move, which raises the risks for drugmakers involved with China. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Duane Wright said:

Though the law didn’t explicitly cover biotechnology, Treasury’s proposed implementing rule would add the sector and prohibit covered investments involving a broad range of pharmaceutical technologies, including small molecules, biologics and cell and gene therapies.

With the Treasury seeking feedback , the final rule might be scaled back. For now, the proposal targets joint ventures and similar transactions rather than instantly prohibiting conventional pharma licensing deals, limiting the immediate risk to drugmakers sourcing Chinese assets, Wright says.

But the proposal adds to the regulatory uncertainty hanging over the biotech sector for investors, and weighs on earnings potential. Any additional clarity in the final rule, coupled with the sector’s outperformance, could provide an additional catalyst for earnings:

The strength of any such recovery remains uncertain. Research and development costs, which temper future EPS growth, patent cliffs, and US drug pricing reforms all cast doubt on revenue projections — but potential efficiency gains from AI should at least offset higher R&D costs.

Additionally, AI research and drug discovery should help offset some of the revenue lost to expiring patents, with an estimated $290 billion to $350 billion in global branded-drug revenue at risk of losing exclusivity between 2025 and 2030 — roughly one-sixth of annual industry revenue.
