Oh Jensen

Some hypotheticals. What if Dario Amodei got caught having an affair with a spy, and another affair with his subordinate at Anthropic PBC? Well. The spy stuff could be bad for Anthropic: Anthropic’s products have serious national security consequences, and there might be regulatory repercussions; its business might be restricted to prevent its models from falling into the wrong hands. I don’t know about the subordinate stuff; I assume a lot of people working in AI live in polyamorous group houses with their coworkers so maybe that’s no big deal.

What would happen to Amodei? I don’t know. But the flowchart is something like this:

  1. Anthropic’s board of directors would meet and decide what to do with him, weighing what various stakeholders — employees, suppliers, customers, governments, shareholders, etc. — want and their own sense of what would be best for the company and for the safe development of advanced artificial intelligence. Anthropic’s board has seven members, two of whom are Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela Amodei, so that might be awkward.
  2. The board ultimately answers to a higher authority . Actually two higher authorities, or two and a half. Three of the seven board members are appointed by a “long-term benefit trust” made up of disinterested trustees whose job is to make sure that Anthropic builds AI safely for the benefit of humanity. Three are appointed by Anthropic’s seven co-founders. The seventh is appointed by the trust, but subject to the approval of the rest of the board, with Anthropic’s shareholders breaking any deadlocks.
  3. So the board would probably put more weight on the views of Anthropic’s co-founders and employees than would a normal public company board. I don’t know what those views would be, in this hypothetical. Maybe all the (other) co-founders and employees feel betrayed by Amodei and want him to go, so he goes. Maybe they all circle the wagons around him and think it is all the more essential that he stay to build safe advanced AI, so he stays. Maybe the founders and employees are ambivalent but the disinterested trustees, who care only about making AI safe for humanity, think that he is [essential/detrimental] to that goal and make the decision on that basis.
  4. And so, in a sense, whatever decision the board makes will have a certain legitimacy: Employees who have devoted their life to Anthropic’s mission will feel like the board (1) is part of that mission and (2) made its decision in accordance with that mission. Customers and suppliers will deal with their usual Anthropic counterparts, who will tell them “nothing has changed and we are stronger than ever,” whether that’s because Amodei stayed or because he left. The Anthropic collective will make its decision in the way that it thinks is best for the Anthropic collective.
  5. Maybe. Lots of ways for this to go wrong. But that is kind of the idea. The reason Anthropic has five classes of stock and two separate boards and convoluted procedures for appointing everybody is to align its formal governance with its founders’ and employees’ current sense of how best to reflect the will of the Anthropic collective. The founders get X amount of power and the employees get Y amount of power and an outside council of wise elders gets Z amount of power and it’s all calibrated to align with how the people at Anthropic think they will want decisions to be made. And the hope is that, if that decision-making process produces a decision like “fire Amodei for scandal” or “keep Amodei despite scandal,” everyone — investors and employees and customers and regulators — will be like “yep, makes sense, that’s Anthropic for you.”

What if Sam Altman got caught having affairs with a spy and a subordinate? I forget the details of OpenAI’s current governance structure, and they’ll be clarified more when it files to go public eventually, but we do kind of have a test case. Back in 2023, OpenAI’s board of directors decided that Altman was not entirely honest with them, so they fired him . That lasted like a weekend. Then investors and employees revolted, Altman came back, most of the board left and the governance structure was drastically revised. Now I suspect the flowchart would be something like what I laid out above for Anthropic, but with considerably more bias toward keeping Altman. Compared to Amodei, Altman has less of a first-among-equals vibe and more of an imperial-CEO vibe, and he has already declined to be fired once. It’s hard to imagine that OpenAI’s current board would dare fire him over a couple of affairs. He’s got the heads of the previous directors on pikes outside his office.

What if Elon Musk got caught having affairs with a spy and a subordinate? Come on!

  1. SpaceX’s governance structure is set up so that Musk controls the company and the board and nobody can do anything about anything. [1] So nobody would do anything about it.
  2. If he thought that his affairs were disqualifying, he could resign in shame, but he absolutely would not think that and would in fact go on X to post proud naughty tweets about the whole thing.
  3. SpaceX’s board of directors and big investors and many politicians would also go on X to post their support for Musk and suggest that it would be irresponsible for a CEO not to have affairs with spies.
  4. This, too, would have a certain legitimacy, wouldn’t it? Like, nobody would change their mind about anything. No employee or customer or supplier or politician who previously thought “ah yes SpaceX is good” would now think “oh no SpaceX is bad.” Everyone kind of knows Elon Musk’s deal, and if he adds some complications to the deal, that will only confirm everyone in their existing views.

But here in the real world, it’s Jensen Huang who got caught having affairs with an alleged spy and a subordinate, and Nvidia Corp. is, relatively speaking, a normal public company with a normal governance structure and a normal board of directors. Two things that that means are:

  1. The board of directors decides what to do with Huang. He is the co-founder and visionary and (until recently) the chief executive officer, but he only owns about 3.4% of the stock and has no special voting rights. As CEO, he answered to the board, but the board did not answer to him.
  2. The board of directors is just, you know, some people . None of them is, or represents, a big investor in Nvidia, or a big customer or supplier for that matter. None of them works (or has worked) at Nvidia. [2] They are independent outsiders with general business and financial expertise whose job is to make good decisions about what is best for Nvidia, balancing the interests of various stakeholders and maximizing long-term shareholder value. But they do not embody the will of the Nvidia collective in quite the way that Anthropic’s complicated series of boards embodies the will of the Anthropic collective, or in the way that Elon Musk’s personal whim embodies the will of the SpaceX collective.

I don’t even mean that this is bad. Arguably it is good. Arguably public-company chief executive officers should be answerable to a reasonably objective, reasonably impartial group of smart experienced grown-ups. Arguably big important public companies should be run on general principles of good governance, not for the aggrandizement of their founders or the delight of their employees.

But, I don’t know, if you’re the board of Nvidia, how can you ever be sure you’re making the right choice? What if you fire Huang and the employees and investors revolt? What if you keep him and the government comes down hard? The Financial Times reports :

Jensen Huang is considering a comeback as Nvidia’s chief executive, as allies in Silicon Valley pressure the company’s board to reinstate him days after the tech executive resigned over reports he had affairs with a suspected Chinese spy and a subordinate.

Huang has discussed with confidants the possibility of returning, according to multiple people familiar with the matter — even as an independent probe by Nvidia’s board investigates the conduct that led to a spectacular leadership crisis at the $4tn chip company this week.

One Huang ally told the FT they were putting together a “war room” of Silicon Valley executives to lobby for the former Nvidia chief’s return and would try to garner support from the White House.

“Everyone that matters in tech outside the frontier labs loves Jensen,” they said. The efforts had Huang’s support, they added.

A public campaign to reinstate Huang has gathered steam over the weekend. Tesla and SpaceX chief Elon Musk and Epic Games chief executive Tim Sweeney were among executives backing a public letter demanding that its board “Bring Jensen Back”.

Huang continues to command fierce loyalty from some employees, several of whom have gone public in calling for his return in the days since he resigned and have signed the open letter.

Altman’s heads on pikes don’t just work to focus the minds of OpenAI’s directors; they also serve as inspiration for other CEOs. Meanwhile Bloomberg reports :

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent … sought on Sunday to close off the possibility of a Huang comeback, calling on Nvidia’s board to go outside the company for new leadership after a series of accusations that it facilitated sales of its advanced artificial intelligence chips to Chinese entities. In recent days, the Treasury chief has also pointed a finger at the Commerce Department for failing to do enough to enforce US export curbs.

“This makes me think that as the board conducts a search for a new leader, that they need to go outside the company,” Bessent said in an interview on Fox News’s Sunday Morning Futures. “They need a dramatic shakeup.” …

In the wake of Jensen Huang’s resignation, Bessent said he wants to know whether Nvidia intentionally ignored warning signs of shell companies, many of them in Singapore, funneling chips to China, signaling he wants to shift the focus from the departed CEO’s personal “foibles” to the legal and national-security aspects.

“I want to keep coming back to what was the culture at Nvidia, and were they pressing these seemingly illicit sales to goose their revenues and their earnings?” Bessent said. “And I think we need answers in the next 10 days from the board and from the outside counsel.”

In principle, the board of directors kind of has a mechanism for balancing all of this stuff. Their job is to look out for the long-term value of the company, so they have to ask questions like “will everyone quit without Jensen” and “will it be bad if everyone quits” and “will Scott Bessent ruin us if we bring Jensen back” and “how much can he do to ruin us” and “ what about President Trump ” and “does it matter that everyone in tech outside the frontier labs loves Jensen” and “does it matter that the frontier labs kind of don’t .” And maybe they throw in some bright-line general rules about good governance and compliance and stuff :

Jeffrey Sonnenfeld, a professor and governance expert at Yale University’s School of Management, said the grounds for Huang’s dismissal were solid under the company’s own code of conduct. “If they were to look the other way, then it’s open season, tear up the rulebook, because nothing is legally enforceable in the company’s own ethical standards,” Sonnenfeld said.

But you can kind of see why everyone in tech wants different, more founder-centric governance structures these days. The big tech CEOs are in some sense almost as big as their companies, and much bigger than their boards of directors. (Certainly bigger than their companies’ ethical standards !) They want a governance structure that reflects that; they can’t quite believe that the will of their companies could be expressed by a vote of independent directors. The simplest approach is founder control, because the founders — and often the employees and investors — tend to think that they are identified with their companies, and their personal decisions embody the will of the company. But that’s not the only approach. Anthropic has a much more nuanced view of what Anthropic wants than “whatever Dario wants,” but it is not a normal board of independent directors either.

Anyway, it looks like the board is not considering bringing Huang back. And I assume that if he did come back, it would be in a fairly conciliatory way in which none of the directors’ heads would end up on pikes. But they’d know not to try anything again.

X-risk and antitrust

Here is how I understand the situation with x-risk and antitrust risk:

  1. The leaders of the big frontier artificial intelligence labs sincerely believe that there is a high probability that their products might kill everyone on earth, and that being slower and more deliberate about product development would meaningfully reduce that probability.
  2. However, each lab finds itself unable to unilaterally slow its product development, because if it slows down and its competitors do not, (1) the competitors will out-compete it for customers, researchers, investors, etc. and (2) the competitors will kill everyone on earth anyway.
  3. The leaders of the big frontier AI labs would be willing to get together in a room and agree to collectively slow down development, and they think that is the only practical way to reduce the probability of killing everyone.
  4. However, when they muse about doing that, their antitrust lawyers jump up and say “NO! You can’t get together in a room with your leading competitors and agree to slow down product rollouts! That is a conspiracy in restraint of trade, and you personally could go to prison for even suggesting it!”
  5. The leaders of the big frontier AI labs do not want to go to prison.
  6. They don’t want to kill everyone on earth either, but the prison stuff is so much more salient.
  7. So they don’t get together in a room to slow development, and instead post essays online saying “hey it would be great if the government made us slow down, or at least waived antitrust law so that we could get together to agree to slow down.”

I’m not sure this is entirely accurate in every respect, and obviously you don’t have to agree with the leaders of the big labs or even think that they are sincere, but I think my summary captures something real about the situation. It is quite irritating for a number of reasons, including in particular No. 6. I feel like standard utilitarianism will tell you that a 10% chance of you personally going to prison is better than a 20% chance of killing everyone on earth!

But here is what I find particularly irritating about it. If Dario Amodei woke up one day and tweeted “I want to pay lower taxes,” an army of extremely smart tax lawyers would arrive at his door within an hour with ideas for how to avoid paying taxes. Everyone understands that there’s a tax code that says you have to pay taxes, but it’s long and complicated and there is a great deal of room for creative structuring, and if you run a trillion-dollar company you can spend, I don’t know, $100 million on very very very good tax advice to achieve your tax goals.

I am not an antitrust expert, but it kind of feels like an analogous situation. Like, if Dario Amodei thinks “I really want to avoid killing everyone on earth, and the only way to do that turns out to be getting in a room with Sam Altman and Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis and agreeing to slow down our product development, but antitrust law says we can’t,” then he should go out and spend, you know, $100 million on antitrust lawyers to try to find a creative structuring solution to get around that problem. I don’t have the solution! This is not legal advice! I cannot sit here and say with certainty that the solution exists. I just, you know. There are all sorts of quasi-regulatory and self-regulatory bodies; there are other industries where companies cooperate in certain ways mediated by regulatory agencies or trade groups or whatever else. Surely you could spend a few tens of millions of dollars of billable time brainstorming?

It just feels like if this is the most important problem facing humanity, then what you want to do is hire many very very good antitrust lawyers and pay them a lot of money to work on it full-time. Not just get a quick first impression from your in-house counsel and be like “ehh, maybe prison, never mind.”

This is a somewhat unsatisfying thing for me to write, because it’s not like I have the answer. But I am at least pleased to see that some people are thinking along similar lines. AT FT Alphaville, Lee Harris writes about one proposal :

Enter the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company, which has identified a weird loophole in US competition law: insurance.

Insurance is exempt from US federal antitrust law, under the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, which gave insurers freedoms including sharing sensitive commercial data on risks and price fixing comparing pricing practices.

AIUC, founded by AI safety researcher Rajiv Dattani, sees an opportunity for the AI industry to use insurance to pool and compare its own risks and incident data.

A jointly owned insurer would receive commercially sensitive incident data, AIUC argues, peer review safety concerns at other labs, and pay claims out of a common fund. …

Similar structures have worked elsewhere. Nuclear energy companies have set up mutuals pooling their liability, and the insurer conducts safety assessments at insured plants. And this seems to have both improved safety and forced the private sector to fund and minimise some of the risks of nuclear catastrophe.

Does this work? I dunno! But this is a good problem for people to be working on, and we need more ideas like this.

Procurement

I suppose that, if you’re a company looking to do a deal with the government, you might want to hire advisers who have experience in doing deals with the government. When you interview potential advisers, you might ask questions like “have you done deals with the relevant agency during the current administration?” and “what sort of relationship do you have with the people currently in charge?” And if they say “oh they love us,” maybe you hire them, and if they say “oh they hate us” or “we’ve never met,” maybe you don’t.

Maybe the advisers proactively make this part of the pitch. But, you know, carefully. They don’t put “the current Secretary of Spending used to work at our firm and still comes to our holiday parties” in the pitchbook. They mention that orally in the pitch meeting.

What doesn’t generally happen, in the US, or so I gather, is that you have a meeting with the government and the person in charge of your deal says “hey, if you want to do this deal, you should hire my buddy as your adviser.” That’s not, like, completely discontinuous with the other stuff here. That’s good advice! “It would be wise for anyone looking to do a big deal to hire advisers with the relevant contacts and expertise”: Who could argue with that? But it does sound bad coming from the government.

Anyway Bloomberg’s Todd Gillespie and Kiel Porter report :

US Deputy Defense Secretary Steve Feinberg steered companies negotiating multibillion-dollar weapons deals with the federal government to his longtime Goldman Sachs Group Inc. banker, according to people familiar with the matter.
Feinberg suggested companies including Lockheed Martin Corp. and RTX Corp. should hire Jim Donovan, a veteran dealmaker at Goldman who for years worked with Feinberg and his former investment firm, Cerberus Capital Management, said the people, who asked not to be named discussing the confidential arrangements. Both of the manufacturers ultimately did so.
Suggesting a banker to the other side of any negotiation is unusual, but it’s all the more so in defense procurement contracts, an arena where Wall Street dealmakers aren’t typically involved. Even if Feinberg’s reference to Donovan was meant as a suggestion or recommendation, some executives decided it would be wise to treat it as a directive, rather than risk complicating the deals, said some of the people. ...
Goldman executives looked into the concerns raised by those clients, one of the people said. After internal discussion, the executives limited the fees that the bank would charge them, some of the people said. ...
Chief Pentagon Spokesperson Sean Parnell called any claims that Feinberg directed companies to use Goldman “categorically false.” Feinberg and the Department of War have “no favorites,” he added.

Disclosure, I used to work at Goldman, but the government never told anyone to hire me.
