Anthropic

Everyone knows that initial public offering orders are kind of fake. A company says it wants to raise $1 billion by selling 100 million shares at $10 each. Its bankers go out to investors, who put in orders for the shares. The bankers collect orders for, say, 700 million shares. “The IPO is seven times oversubscribed,” they say. [1] The IPO prices, and the bankers and company get together to “allocate” the IPO: There are orders for 700 million shares, but the company is selling only 100 million shares (actually 115 million with the greenshoe ), so most of the orders won’t get filled. The banks and company will favor some investors (long-term investors who understand the company’s story, the banks’ favorite clients, retail, whatever) and zero others (fast-money hedge funds that will flip the shares, etc.), but even the favored investors will probably get cut back. If you put in an order for 10 million shares, and the book is seven times covered, you’re not realistically going to get more than 5 million, and 2 million is more likely.

But you know that, of course. If you have a sense that it will be a hot IPO — for instance because your salesperson at the bank says “this is a hot IPO and we’re getting a lot of demand” — you can scale up your order to try to get more shares. If you want 5 million shares, put in an order for 20 million. That way you’ll probably get cut back to 3 or 4 or 5 or 6 million. Sometimes you’ll mis-calibrate and end up with 1 million, or 10 million, either of which would be bad. (If you get 1 million, you’ll have to buy more when the stock starts trading, and if you got cut back to 1 million that probably means it will trade up. If you get 10 million, the reverse: You’ll have to sell when the stock starts trading, and if you got allocated so many shares, that’s a bad sign for demand.) But in a hot IPO, many investors are putting in orders for more shares than they want. [2]

Or that is the stereotype. I have sometimes wondered if it applies to the new wave of mega-IPOs. Bloomberg’s Bailey Lipschultz reports today :

Anthropic PBC is on the cusp of pricing potentially the biggest IPO of all time, defying days of volatility in technology stocks that threaten to weigh on the startup’s long-awaited debut.

The AI model maker is guiding prospective investors in its initial public offering that it expects to price the shares above the range, according to a person familiar with the matter, who asked not to be identified as the information isn’t public. A spokesperson for Anthropic couldn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.

Anthropic and its seven co-founders are marketing 825 million shares in aggregate for $70 to $78 each, according to its filings. At the top of the price range, and including a $26 billion private placement, the IPO would raise $90.4 billion — topping SpaceX’s record $75 billion base deal — and give the company a fully diluted valuation of about $1.4 trillion.

Demand is cresting, with bankers briefing that the IPO is close to seven times oversubscribed, people familiar with the matter have said.

“Seven times oversubscribed” means that the banks have orders for something like $500 billion worth of stock. That’s a … lot? Lipschultz notes that “IPOs on US exchanges have raised $165 billion” in 2026 so far, “versus the all-time high of $195 billion reached in 2021”; the last few years were much lighter. The demand for Anthropic shares roughly equals the total amount raised in all US IPOs over the last six years. Seems high.

Or, put another way, if Anthropic was added to the S&P 500 Index tomorrow (it won’t be ), that would create demand for on the order of $336 billion of Anthropic stock from index funds. [3] The IPO demand is much more than that.

Back when companies went public relatively early and IPOs were relatively small, you could say “I want 10 million shares” when you only wanted 5 million, and it was basically fine. Occasionally you’d get filled on all 10 million and have to move some money around to actually buy them. But each IPO was a small part of your portfolio, and being a little off was not a huge deal.

But now investors are putting in, you know, $400 billion of orders for Anthropic stock that won’t get filled. It’s just a weirder dynamic. The Wall Street Journal notes :

Altimeter founder Brad Gerstner said on CNBC Monday that his firm requested $5 billion worth of shares.

Investors in IPOs typically submit orders above what they hope to receive, knowing that for high-demand offerings like Anthropic they are unlikely to receive their full requests.

Altimeter reports $18 billion of assets under management and owns about $10.3 billion of public stocks. It put in an order for … half its portfolio … in Anthropic? Seems high. What if it got filled? Presumably it won’t, but what if it did?

Anyway, right, Anthropic prices this evening and starts trading tomorrow. It plans to price above the $70-to-$78 marketing range for the IPO, despite a rough week or two for AI stocks. (This is not a huge surprise: It launched at a fairly conservative valuation, so it had some room to go up even as other stocks went down.) It might upsize the deal to sell more shares.

How will it trade? The normal answer to that question is “it will trade up about 20% from the IPO price” — that’s what most banks and companies target — but there is a wide range of uncertainty around that expectation. We talked a few weeks ago about the pre-IPO perpetual futures market, an offshore betting market that might or might not do a good job predicting the trading prices of companies that are about to go public. The perps predict the normal outcome, Bloomberg’s Phil Serafino reports :

Crypto futures tied to the AI lab’s stock market debut have fallen alongside AI stocks, but they still imply a blockbuster valuation of about $1.7 trillion. That’s well above the $1.4 trillion the company would be worth at the top of its proposed IPO price range.

The perpetual futures contracts give investors an early read on speculative appetite, though those willing to place the bets aren’t necessarily the ones who will buy the shares.

SpaceX’s debut in June showed the limits of that signal. Its shares opened at $150, above the $135 offer price but well below the roughly $176 indicated by crypto futures.

Anthropic’s trading debut tomorrow will give investors another chance to see how close the crypto market comes to the opening price.

“The perps market is still positioned for a pop, just a smaller one than it was pricing a week ago,” said Kelly Ye of Talos.

One possibility is that the perps market reflects the wisdom of the crowd and the expectations of the market, and that the actual trading price tomorrow will be closely continuous with the perps price today. Another possibility is that the perps market reflects the conventional wisdom that the expected IPO pop is 20%, so the perps are trading 20% above the expected IPO price.

Trump nickname markets

Back in June, the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission proposed new federal rules for sports gambling. The CFTC regulates commodity futures markets, prediction markets like Kalshi are technically commodity futures markets, and they list a lot of sports bets. The CFTC has not historically been in the business of regulating sports bets, so it was writing federal sports-bets rules on a blank sheet of paper.

As I wrote at the time , the CFTC has a somewhat different focus from most state gaming regulators. Gaming regulators are typically in the consumer protection business; they want to make sure that bets are paid out fairly and that people don’t get too addicted to gambling. The CFTC is mostly in the market integrity business: It doesn’t care that much about gambling addiction, but it doesn’t want its markets to be full of manipulation or insider trading.

So the CFTC’s sports betting rules have a market-integrity flavor to them. They encourage bets on things like the outcomes of professional and college sporting events, but discourage bets on things like:

  • the outcomes of Little League games,
  • whether the next pitch is a ball or a strike,
  • whether a player will make a shot or commit a foul,
  • whether a player will be injured, or when she will recover from injury, and
  • whether a game will break out in a brawl.

These bets are problematic because they could “create perverse financial incentives” to manipulate the outcomes, because they can be manipulated by the actions of just one or two people, and because knowledge of these things might be held by a small group of people who could insider trade on them. (Little League games are rarely televised, an injured player’s status is known mostly by her team and her doctors, etc.)

I am being slightly tongue-in-cheek when I say that the CFTC proposed sports gambling rules. That is mostly true, but technically the CFTC regulates prediction markets, and prediction markets offer event contracts beyond sports bets. A similar analysis should apply to other event contracts. Any sort of event contract that is readily subject to manipulation, or that creates perverse incentives, or that concerns events known only to a small group of people, would be bad. As I wrote in June :

It sure seems to me like “mention markets” — bets on whether some public figure will say a particular word in a public appearance — would be contrary to the public interest under the CFTC’s reasoning, as “insight into the underlying event is highly concentrated — in a single individual, for example, or only individuals legally prohibited from transacting.” (“Will George Santos attend the State of the Union ,” similarly.) They are obviously ripe for insider trading or manipulation, and don’t seem to serve much public purpose. But the CFTC doesn’t specifically discuss them, so it’s hard to be sure.

And in fact, in September, the CFTC put out a staff advisory concluding that mention markets “may be viewed as ‘presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation.’” And Bloomberg’s Bobby Allyn reported yesterday :

Federal regulators ordered Kalshi Inc. to halt trading on markets tied to what President Donald Trump will say during public events, in the latest sign that prediction markets are coming under greater scrutiny after the midterm elections.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission notified the company on Friday that all so-called mention markets related to the president are under a formal stay, forcing an indefinite pause on further trading. The CFTC said in its order that it was examining whether the contracts were “readily susceptible to manipulation.”

Here is the CFTC’s order , which concerns “the listing of the ‘What nicknames will Trump say before 2027?’ contract and the TRUMPSAY contract series” on Kalshi:

On September 22, 2026, staff of the Division of Market Oversight (“DMO”) issued a Staff Advisory on Individual Mention, Attendance and Interaction Event Contracts (the “Staff Advisory”). The Staff Advisory, which concerned “the listing and trading of event contracts based on whether an individual will say or ‘mention’ certain words, attend or appear at an event, or otherwise interact with another person,” concluded that such contracts may be viewed as “presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation,” because they “may be controlled by a single individual, a small group of individuals, or persons with access to or influence over the individual whose words, attendance, or interaction determines settlement.” The Staff Advisory recognized that “whether a contract is readily susceptible to manipulation is a contract-specific determination and necessarily involves consideration of not only the design of the particular contract but also the effectiveness of any prophylactic measures implemented by the DCM to prevent manipulation and misappropriation of material non-public information.” ...

The Contracts are “Mention Markets” per the Staff Advisory, as they are “based on whether an individual will say or ‘mention’ certain words.” Notwithstanding, roughly a week after the Staff Advisory was released, Kalshi listed the Contract for trading without amending the Contract Series’ self-certification (or submitting a new self-certification) to make the “heightened showing” of non-manipulability required by the Staff Advisory.

Two points here. First, yes, bets on “what nicknames will Trump say before 2027” are “susceptible to manipulation” because those nicknames are “controlled by a single individual.” But they are … susceptible to manipulation … by … Trump? The CFTC’s thesis here is “Kalshi has insufficient safeguards in place to prevent the guy saying these nicknames from saying these nicknames to enrich himself or his friends.” But the guy saying the nicknames is the president of the United States, and the CFTC’s boss. And the CFTC seems to have shut down only Trump’s mention markets. You can still go on Kalshi and bet on what Zohran Mamdani will say at the DealBook Summit, or what Walmart will say on its next earnings call, or even what Trump’s press secretary will say at the next press briefing. The one person whose mentions markets are too hot to handle is Donald Trump himself. Is that like:

  1. The CFTC thinks Trump is uniquely likely to manipulate these markets, or be manipulated himself, compared with anyone else including his own press secretary?
  2. The CFTC thinks “persons with access to or influence over” Trump are uniquely likely to manipulate these markets? (His former teleprompter operator did already get in trouble for insider trading on Trump mention markets, so it’s a reasonable thing to think.)
  3. The CFTC thinks that manipulation of presidential nickname markets is uniquely dangerous and therefore wants to shut it down even if the probability of manipulation is low?

One possible answer is “all of the above,” but I am not sure the CFTC can say that.

Second, Kalshi is deeply committed to its somewhat comical position that absolutely any bet you can think of is a legitimate hedge for some real business risk, so it wants to keep listing these contracts. Allyn reports:

Tarek Mansour, Kalshi’s chief executive officer, said at an Axios event on Tuesday that he disagreed with the CFTC’s move and defended the trading.

“What the president does say moves markets,” Mansour said at the event. “So predicting what he’s about to say is actually pretty meaningful.”

And, you know what, sure. There probably is some correlation between, like, the price of oil and whether Trump starts using a rude nickname for Mojtaba Khamenei . Hedge your geopolitical risk in the mentions markets, sure, sure, sure sure sure, sure. But here is the actual “ What nicknames will Trump say before 2027? ” contract, [4] with actual bets on terms like “Crying Chuck,” “Fat Slob,” “Piggy,” “Slopadopolous,” “Marjorie Traitor Greene,” “Talafreako” and “Comrade Kamala.” Oh go ahead! Construct the scenario where you are a small business and your sales will suffer if Trump does or does not say “Slopadopolous”! [5] “I run a family-owned political merch store, and I have the confidence to print 5,000 ‘Slopadopolous’ T-shirts because I can hedge my risk of irrelevance on Kalshi.” I hope they’re making that ad now .

Fake takeover

We talk from time to time around here about fake takeover bids. There is an obvious explanation for fake takeover bids: You buy the stock of a public company, you put out a fake press release saying that some private equity firm wants to buy the company at a premium, the stock goes up, you sell your shares and profit. It’s a classic market manipulation.

But my impression has always been that a lot of people in the fake takeover game are not in it purely for the money. My impression is that a lot of people just think it would be very fun to send a public company a term sheet for a takeover, and get back a counterproposal from the company, and negotiate the terms of the takeover, and submit financing commitments from banks and legal opinions from lawyers, and generally play-act all of the steps of high finance. Actually doing that with real money for a real company requires either (1) having the money or (2) getting a selective and demanding job at a professional services firm that does mergers and acquisitions. But pretending to do it requires only a vivid imagination and some facility with Microsoft Word.

Is negotiating a pretend takeover fun? I mean. I have never done it. (I have been involved in negotiating real takeovers and, honestly, yes, kind of fun.) I realize that most people would not find it fun. But I do think that I would find it fun. So I sympathize with the people who actually do it, for fun.

Most of them also do it for profit, fine, yes, but sometimes there are tells that they mostly want the fun. Like here is how you do a fake takeover to maximize profit:

  1. Buy stock.
  2. Issue fake press release.
  3. Sell stock.

Not legal advice, but quite straightforward. The company never needs to be involved: You are doing a fraud on the stock market , and the key element of it is the press release . Here, on the other hand, is how you do a fake takeover to maximize your own idiosyncratic role-playing enjoyment:

  1. Buy stock.
  2. Send the company a confidential letter offering to buy it.
  3. Get back a skeptical response from the company asking for more details.
  4. Submit supporting documents from your (imaginary) bankers and lawyers.
  5. Arrange a meeting.
  6. Arrive at the meeting in a suit and say lots of fun mergers-and-acquisitions words.
  7. Promise to follow up with additional documents.
  8. String this along as long as possible.
  9. Forget to announce the fake takeover or sell the stock, because you’re having so much fun and don’t want it to end.

I don’t want to claim that everyone doing fake takeovers is doing only this, but some people doing fake takeovers are clearly doing some of this. Last week, Bloomberg’s Isla Binnie and Lucca de Paoli reported :

A man who posed as a private equity executive forged documents and faked an American accent to launch a bogus £154 million ($206 million) bid for a commodities company in a bid to push up the share price was convicted of fraud.

Christopher Woolcott, 44, was handed a two-year suspended sentence over fraud and forgery at Southwark Crown Court in London on Tuesday after pleading guilty to the Financial Conduct Authority charges.

Woolcott attempted to increase the value of Touchstone Exploration Inc after buying a number of shares in both the Toronto and London stock exchanges. The day-trader created a series of personas including a private equity executive, a lawyer and a message-board account called “Junkie Monkey” to make his fake bid, the court was told on Tuesday. …

In late November 2024 Touchstone management were approached by someone saying they were Simon Jones of Griffon Partners wanting to discuss a takeover offer for the company. Jones then sent Touchstone’s chief executive officer an email from a personal email address, something he said was due to a malware attack that Griffon Partners had recently suffered. In reality, this was Woolcott assuming a fake identity. ...

The company’s CEO met with Woolcott at the May Fair Hotel on Stratton Street in December 2024, with Woolcott this time representing himself as Jones’s partner Greg Thomas and used an American accent, described as a “Boston drawl.”

In his attempt to trick Touchstone into believing the bid was genuine, Woolcott forged a proof of funds letter from HSBC Holdings Plc, faked a solicitor letter from RSW Law and used ChatGPT to help create the forgeries. He then used Discord accounts to leak pictures of some of the falsified documents, according to the prosecutors.

So he leaked pictures on Discord but did not … put out a press release? Touchstone’s stock mostly went down in late November and December 2024. Here is the FCA’s announcement , which notes that, “Had his plan succeeded, he stood to gain as much as £70,000 through the sale of his shares.” I feel like he could have done some things to increase the chances of his plan succeeding?

Was he in it for the money? Sure, yes, but (1) not in a particularly efficient way and (2) not exclusively. Binnie and de Paoli note:

The judge also referred to a psychologist’s report that had been submitted with evidence, in which Woolcott indicated find it “exciting to be pretend to be a different character” and getting “carried away”.

I just think that that’s always the main explanation for fake takeovers, and the money is secondary.

Everything is securities fraud

If Nvidia Corp. secretly worked with a Chinese spy to send advanced artificial-intelligence chips to China in ways that got around US export controls, is that bad? Is it illegal ? Is it light treason ? I don’t know, but there is one thing that I do know, which is that everything is securities fraud. We’ve talked already about the private lawsuits , but the Wall Street Journal reports :

The Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating Nvidia, whose star chief executive Jensen Huang recently resigned after revelations of extramarital affairs, one with a suspected Chinese spy and another with an employee, according to people familiar with the matter.

The civil investigation adds to the crisis facing Nvidia, whose shares have fallen more than 10% since Huang was ousted. The focus of the investigation couldn’t immediately be learned but includes whether the company’s disclosures misled investors, some of the people said.

I have written before that the SEC is an all-purpose meta-regulator. “Should we sell advanced AI chips to China” is a contested political question about which even the Trump administration disagrees ; Congress and the Commerce Department and the intelligence community all have their views and proposals, and it is not clear who gets to decide. But if it involves a public company’s stock falling, the SEC gets to decide.

Elsewhere: “The Denny’s where Nvidia was born removed the sign honoring the company.”
