  • It’s not a “one and done” but the start of a hiking cycle .
  • EM currencies hit their lowest since March as the dollar rallied .

A Discipline, Not Just a Decision

Heading into Wednesday’s Federal Open Market Committee meeting, it was a truth almost universally acknowledged that the central bank would raise rates. New Chair Kevin Warsh’s speech last month at Jackson Hole appeared to leave him no choice. Many were unconvinced a hike was necessary, and expected Warsh to draw the sting by hinting this would be “one and done,” not the start of a hiking cycle.

He didn’t do that. By the close, markets were convinced that more hikes would come, and that Warsh was happy about it. A potentially contentious meeting ended with unusual clarity.

Why so convinced? Key clues were:

  1. The decision to hike was unanimous (unusual of late).
  2. The “dot plot” of projections showed FOMC members expected to hike at least once more this year.
  3. Warsh framed the hike as part of a “discipline” and “the removal of some accommodation” — implying more to come.
  4. Warsh and the FOMC all presented the economy as stronger than they had thought — classic conditions for more tightening.

Perhaps the single clearest indication of intent was the dot plot , which Warsh dislikes . The other members submitted projections that showed their average predicted fed funds rate for the end of the year had risen 25 basis points since the last dot plot in June. Their estimate for the end of 2027 rose half a percentage point:

The accompanying statement revised the previous version from July subtly but clearly in a hawkish direction . Here it is, with changes annotated in red by Oxford Economics:

References to supply shocks are out, while there is now a broader nod to geopolitics rather than the specifics of the war with Iran. Then came the Warsh press conference, in which reporters weren’t allowed to ask follow-ups to their questions. It was truncated to 29 minutes from the usual 45 , but the way stocks and bonds traded during his brief back-and-forth showed that he’d had enough time to make his point:

The biggest reaction was in foreign exchange, portending confidence in the Fed but also a tightening of financial conditions in the emerging world. The JPMorgan EM FX index of emerging currencies dropped to its lowest since March, while Bloomberg’s broad dollar index enjoyed its best day since Warsh’s first FOMC meeting sparked a rally three months ago:

To some extent, the key was what Warsh didn’t say, rather than what he did. There was no suggestion that this was a one-off. Instead, he said the FOMC has “removed a dose of accommodation” — not all of it, implying the rate was still accommodative and that there would be more doses in the prescription. He added that the hike “starts to show we’re serious about this.” If that was only a start to the seriousness, the direct implication is to brace for more.

As a result, the market priced in a lot of tightening. Implicit fed funds rates, as gauged by the Bloomberg World Interest Rate Probabilities, were projected to peak below 4% when Warsh took office in May — at a point when the market had already had time to adjust to the energy shock from the Iran war. Expectations crept up a bit by the time he spoke at Jackson Hole; and since then the market has braced for three more hikes before the Fed is done:

This turnabout is made all the more remarkable by the fact that inflation has declined since Warsh took over. There’s no obvious change in the environment in that time to force the central bank to shift direction. It also, of course, runs directly counter to the express wishes of President Donald Trump, who nominated him. He responded to the news with a social media post calling for rates to come down to 1% .

Trump didn’t, however, attack Warsh by name, and in comments to reporters appeared to suggest that his chairman was the prisoner of a hostile committee: “You might as well vote with the board because it’s not going to matter. The board is very hostile, very political.”

That’s not the way everyone else saw it, with the unanimity widely interpreted as showing that Warsh was beginning to exert control. Steven Englander of Standard Chartered PLC, who earlier cautioned that a hike wasn’t necessary, explained how the chairman convinced investors that it was his idea to tighten further:

Warsh constantly emphasised the strength of the US economy and the robustness of US labour markets, indicating that inflation — not activity — was the Fed’s focus. This assessment, and his downplaying of the dot plot that showed only two hikes, may have convinced the market that it had low-balled his willingness to hike. The unanimous vote may have contributed to a sense that this is now the Warsh FOMC.

Given the Fed’s stark change of direction, and the heated political environment, the market reaction was remarkable chiefly for its moderation. Stocks fell, but by the close the S&P 500 had shed only 0.45% while the Nasdaq 100, home of the big tech groups that lead this bull market, was flat — a non-reaction that implies investors don’t think the AI boom has much to fear from a hiking cycle. The 10-year yield rose to 5.02%, but it had gone slightly higher on Tuesday. This wasn’t the much-feared disorderly bond selloff, implying some degree of confidence in Warsh.

In the longer term, this moment could yet seem more significant. It’s a big step away from the “new normal” conditions that followed the Global Financial Crisis, and implies the destination is not the pre-crisis “old normal” driven by globalization and favorable demographics. Salman Ahmed of Fidelity International argued:

The point is not simply that inflation is taking longer to fall. The structure of the economy has changed. Geopolitical fragmentation, energy security, larger fiscal footprints, supply-chain duplication, and the capital intensity of the AI investment cycle all point towards a world in which inflation is likely to remain more persistent.

The calm reception so far shows investors’ acceptance that the world is more expensive and could require higher rates. That calm can continue if bonds move in orderly fashion, and whether the strengthening dollar drives crises in the many countries that have big overhangs of government debt.

The Need for Clarity

Bitcoin investors were seriously disappointed by the US Senate’s inability to advance the Clarity Act — legislation aimed at providing clear legal parameters for the crypto industry (or, to its critics , make life far too easy for crypto scammers). Their disappointment is understandable. Cryptocurrency had been enjoying a rare good spell, driven predominantly by renewed concerns about debasement after Scott Bessent’s Treasury buyback announcement, which prompted a 30% rebound in a matter of days.

But it never regained all its losses for the year, and the Senate vote not to move the Clarity Act forward knocked the wind out of Bitcoin’s sails again:

For crypto enthusiasts, this setback stings. Until Bessent’s announcement, Bitcoin had been in the doldrums, and losing support from institutional investors. The return of the debasement trade had raised the prospect of a sustained rally.

Before the legislation failed, Bitcoin exchange-traded fund flows had surged, attracting $3.5 billion in August and another $600 million so far in September. Bloomberg Intelligence’s analysis shows that Bitcoin funds have attracted a cumulative $55 billion since the launch over two years ago, down from a high of $63 billion.

However, the latest tumble by more than 4% underscores how much investors needed the Clarity Act. To quote Bitwise Asset Management’s Matthew Hougan, “If Clarity had passed, crypto would have been the consensus smart money trade in Q4.”

With Clarity failing, it’s a more challenging market. I am still bullish we’d end the year higher, but it would be a bit slower. I don’t think it delays the bull market, it’s more of a speed bump than a road stop.

This administration continues to be the most crypto-friendly by an awesome distance. Still, some of the Senate sticking points should have investors questioning its staying power. Democrats raised concerns over ethics provisions concerning governing officials’ crypto holdings, an apparent dig at the Trump family’s huge stake in crypto.

It’s unclear how much that affected the vote. It’s plausible that this is not the end of the road, especially if Republicans make concessions as they did last year with the GENIUS Act — which established a regulatory framework for issuing stablecoins and also failed its first cloture vote. But as Frnt Financial’s Stephane Ouellette notes, the two scenarios are slightly different:

Timing also compounds the challenge of passing the bill: The Senate has roughly 22 working days left before midterm campaigning takes over. However, some politicians signaled that an opportunity to pass the bill in this session of Congress still exists.

How realistic are the odds that the Clarity Act sees the light of day? Prediction markets give a very slim probability that it ever passes. Regardless, Trump’s crypto empire and his commitment to the asset class suggest that there will likely be more policy tailwinds from Washington.



Meanwhile, the war in the Middle East could determine crypto’s trajectory. Bitcoin hasn’t worked as a safe haven. It’s hard to see how it could weather a possible escalation, particularly one that coincides with tighter monetary policy.

Of course, enthusiasts have seen worse. Between 2017 and 2020, Bitcoin went more than 1,100 days without making an all-time high. The current drought stands at less than 350.
