So Much Owed to So Few

As ever, Winston Churchill has just the quotation for the hour. In 1940, after a few brave young fighter pilots had managed to hold back the Luftwaffe in the Battle of Britain and delay a planned German invasion, he said : “Never in the field of human conflict has so much been owed by so many to so few.”

In rather less stirring times and with — thankfully — much less at stake, his words now apply to the US stock market. The Nasdaq 100, home of all the big tech stocks with which the US has led the world, closed Tuesday at an all-time high, its first since June. The many who invest there through index funds and ETFs can be thankful, as never before, to a very few companies. Market breadth has never been this narrow, and yet the domination by a handful of stocks continues to intensify.

Half of Tuesday’s rise in the Nasdaq was attributable to four chipmaking companies — Micron Technology Inc., Sandisk Corp., Advanced Micro Devices Inc. and Nvidia Corp. The 6.4% rally of the last week to bring the index to that record came even though 32 members suffered falls. Half of the Nasdaq’s gain over those five days came from just five stocks — Micron, AMD, Nvidia, Intel Corp. and Meta Platforms Inc.

The concentration isn’t new. Since the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 ushered in the excitement over artificial intelligence, the standard version of the Nasdaq 100, in which each stock is weighted according to its market capitalization, has beaten the equal-weight version, a measure of the “average stock” in which each constituent counts for 1%, to a remarkable degree:

It’s not normally like this. In the longer term, smaller companies tend to outperform. That’s not surprising, as they carry more risk (which needs to be compensated) and they have more room to grow. Buying the biggest stock in an index is usually a bad idea, as its value has been fully recognized and the only way (at least relative to others) is down. Those who bought International Business Machines Corp. and General Electric Co., which both had long stretches as the largest US stock, could have done much better.

Extended periods of cap-weighted outperformance, like the current one, are very unusual. The most similar episode was in the late 1990s. The market’s narrowing during the AI boom looks very much like the rally once the Yahoo IPO set off the dot-com bubble. It’s not a great precedent:

The statistics on breadth grow more disconcerting as you dig into them. Jason Goepfert, the founder of SentimenTrader, took to X to call Monday’s action “crazy stuff”:

There are two days in history like today, when the S&P 500 rallied at least 1% to within 1% of a new high, and more of its stocks fell to new lows than highs. July 23, 1929, and December 21, 1999.

Both dates fell three months before epic market collapses. The sample size is far too small to prove anything, but the numbers do establish that these are strange circumstances indeed.

There’s at least one good fundamental reason why the Nasdaq is so narrow. To an unprecedented extent, companies have been allowed to build dominant platforms and keep the profits from them. That has meant that revenues and profits have congregated in a small group. Overall, that leads to extraordinary growth in earnings expectations for the index, but these are in fact dependent on a handful of leaders:

It’s the nature of capitalism that there will always be a few big winners at any one time. It’s also possible that breadth could return via broad gains for the rest, rather than a 1929- or 2000-like crash for the leaders. But the narrowness of this advance should dampen any excitement over the latest Nasdaq record.

A Rare Meeting

The fraught US-China relationship gets its latest test in Washington this week as Xi Jinping arrives for a state visit with Donald Trump. For all their differences, this meeting, coming after last year’s tariff offensive was blunted by Beijing’s leverage over key rare-earth minerals , underscores that neither president can do without the other. Efforts at self-sufficiency continue, but the world’s two largest economies remain interdependent. The pageantry will not mask the tensions.


This adversarial relationship does, however, seem to have an unusual point of consensus: Both oppose calls to slow the intensifying AI race . Neither Washington nor Beijing is willing to do anything that might cede supremacy to the other. Beyond mistrust, Xi’s resistance is understandable, as technology is a bright spot in an increasingly weak economy — and indeed Chinese tech stocks have even outperformed US rivals of late:

Chinese tech stocks’ stellar run dates back to the “whatever it takes” moment two years ago when the Communist Party seemed to commit to creating a consumption-driven economy. That apparent policy pivot proved short-lived, and the broader market has given up all its gains since then. At this point, a move to apply the brakes would be like pulling the rug from under the entire economy:

Continued malaise in domestic demand has unsettled Xi. Andy Rothman of Sinology LLC expects him to prioritize Chinese consumers in the discussions in Washington:

This won’t be as difficult as many believe: Prior to 2020, consumption was the main engine of China’s economic growth. When entrepreneur and consumer confidence returns, they can draw down some of the massive savings increase from 2020. It will take time, but this is a great opportunity for investors to better understand the consumption story.

Henry Huiyao Wang of the Beijing-based Center for China and Globalization argues that neither country can afford to sideline discussions about guardrails. He suggests that their agreement in 2024 that humans must control decisions involving nuclear weapons would be a good starting point:

I propose that we adopt a reciprocal no-first-use commitment covering AI-enabled autonomous weapons... Under this commitment, neither country nor any future signatories would be the first to deploy an autonomous AI system capable of initiating harm autonomously; instead, an accountable human must remain in the loop.

Barring voluntary restraint by frontier AI model developers, the low level of trust between the countries undercuts hope of additional cooperation. Tina Fordham of Fordham Global Foresight expects little from Thursday’s meeting:

Possibly a narrower tariff truce but potentially rising compliance costs from new US technology restrictions. China continues to be the beneficiary of US overreach and military overstretch, and is increasingly being called upon to intervene in conflict resolution.

Beijing’s quest for a workaround to the ban on some US AI chips is far from straightforward, with reports of Chinese companies circumventing American export controls through physical chip smuggling, third-country front companies, and remote access to cloud infrastructure. These efforts further undermine trust.

However, Mitch Presnick of the Asia Society’s Center for China Analysis argues that while “there is a far stronger exclusionary instinct in both capitals than there is a cooperation instinct,” collaboration can’t be ruled out entirely:

Both countries actually benefit from having the other there, whether it’s cooperative or not, because not all symbiosis is voluntary or cooperative, but it’s always a balanced state.

Both leaders have equally compelling reasons to keep their cards close to their chests. It’s hard not to be cynical. In an outcome that could be good for markets this week, but not so great for the long term, Peter Tchir of Academy Securities suggests that China could “say all the right things, and then continue to do whatever they were going to do anyways.”

—Richard Abbey

Whatever It Took

Last week’s tightening by the Federal Reserve , and move above 5% for the 10-year Treasury yield, inevitably had global knock-on effects. It’s time for a periodic reminder that the trends of rising bond yields , increasing political polarization, and growing concerns over fiscal deficits still appear to be heading for a crisis intersection in France.

The spread between the yields payable on 10-year French and German bonds is now even wider — the clearest expression of the market’s perception of the political risk in Paris. German politics looks its most volatile in generations, with Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s party failing to reach even the 5% threshold in a state election last week as the hard-right Alternative für Deutschland made further inroads. And still for the market, the extra French risk is now above one full percentage point, its highest since early 2012:

That landmark is doubly significant, as the latest widening also took French spreads above their level from the eve of the July 2012 speech by the then-president of the European Central Bank, Mario Draghi, in which he promised to do “whatever it takes” to save the euro.

It marked the beginning of the end of Europe’s sovereign debt crisis. Now, the bond market is at least implying that it will take even more. Ideally, that will involve France’s politicians finally thrashing out a credible way to rein in their deficit. Alternatively, the experience from 2012 suggests that the pressure on the ECB to do more could grow as well.

Survival Tips

Getting back to Churchill, it’s worth listening to the audio of his speeches from 1940, when he was new to the job and defeat looked very likely. They’re familiar but still extraordinary. Listen to “Blood, toil, tears and sweat,” “We will fight them on the beaches,” and “Their finest hour.” In contemporary parlance, he held barely any cards, but nobody ever talked a better fight.
