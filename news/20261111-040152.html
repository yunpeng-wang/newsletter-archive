An Information-Rich Inflation Report

October turns out to have been an information-rich month, as Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh would put it. The new information, in the form of inflation data for October, suggests that the Fed probably should have hiked last month, as many said at the time. Warsh tortured logic to justify the inaction, though it seemed clear that avoiding Trumpian attack before the midterms was a factor. However, these figures make abundantly obvious why voters were so angry when they went to the polls last week.

Year-on-year data aren’t available because the government shutdown nixed an inflation report for last October. But the 11-month inflation rate, from November, showed a rise on last month, while the 13-month rate topped 4%:

Month-on-month measures of core inflation — the trimmed mean excluding outliers, and the median — both also suggested that October saw a sharp increase that could not be attributed to oil prices:

Food inflation, which has grown politically sensitive in recent years, and services inflation excluding shelter — a number dubbed the “supercore” by former chairman Jerome Powell — also moved higher:

Unlike some recent bad inflation prints, this one couldn’t be blamed on an obvious outlier. As Barclays’ Pooja Sriram pointed out, the increase ranged across furniture, appliances, household supplies, toys, televisions, smartphones, laptops and PCs, cosmetics, and even prescription drugs.

In our view, this side of the report carries clear imprints of freight costs spilling over into consumer prices, as well as continued AI-related inflation.

All of this hit particularly badly after Fed Governor Christopher Waller , who last year was dissenting in favor of rate cuts , gave a speech in London suggesting that a “jumbo” hike of 50 basis points should be a possibility. Saying he supported a 25-basis-point hike next month, he predicted that inflation for October would come in at about 0.3%, implying that the annual rate would edge closer to 4%:

If inflation comes in substantially higher than I expect, more robust action by the FOMC may be called for. September’s inflation data were bad. There was no silver lining.

It’s not clear whether Waller meant to hint at a 50-basis-hike in one go, or just a further hike after December. After the data, Lorie Logan , head of the Dallas Fed, directly proposed the latter:

It remains clear to me, given the current outlook, that the FOMC needs to implement at least two more 25-basis-point rate increases.

Neither Waller nor Logan dissented in favor of hikes last month, so the wholly reasonable response to this concertedly hawkish Fedspeak was to price in a hike for next month as a total certainty. This is how the expected number of hikes by the end of 2026, and by September next year, has moved since the Fed raised rates in September:

There’s now an 11% chance of a jumbo hike next month, so the market still does not take that idea terribly seriously — but the notional terminal rate for this cycle continues to be all over the place. Last week, the market doubted there would be even two more hikes in total; now three is regarded as a certainty.

The predictable result was to upend the bond market, again. The two-year Treasury yield, particularly sensitive to rate expectations, had its biggest daily rise since the reversal after the Liberation Day tariffs last April:

Meanwhile, the 10-year yield had appeared poised to plummet after long bonds grew heavily oversold at the end of last month. Instead, the yield hit the bottom of the marked upward trend it had formed for the year, and bounced higher. A failed attempt to break out of a trend like this could impact market psychology:

Easing bond yields, in combination with the falling oil price, have relieved pressure on a range of risk assets around the world in the last few weeks, and so it was perhaps reassuring that US stocks were relatively unscathed by this news. The S&P 500 fell 0.38%, while its equal-weighted version was virtually flat, easing only 0.02%.

So maybe one other useful piece of information for Warsh and his colleagues is that the stock market might be prepared to tolerate rate rises — if they head off longer yields at the scary heights of a month ago, and if they really do rein in inflation, which appears to be entrenched once more as a problem.

Anthropic’s New Dawn

Anthropic PBC’s imminent blockbuster initial public offering, we now know, will seek to raise up to $90.4 billion. That’s a lot of money, but it’s grown even more in significance thanks to timing: It arrives just as investors are worrying about the finance for artificial intelligence and grow concerned about tighter regulation.

The scale of Anthropic’s ambition is not in doubt. Its target includes a concurrent private placement, at a fully diluted valuation of about $1.4 trillion , according to its latest filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. The combined fundraising would surpass the $75 billion raised by Elon Musk’s SpaceX in June, and set a new record for public-market financing:

Likely rate hikes make the path murkier. As arguably the most consequential AI IPO to date – the first frontier lab to enter public markets — the stakes are high. The company’s revenue growth over the last three years, as illustrated by Bloomberg colleagues below, is phenomenal — but it must show that it can raise the money successfully if investors’ confidence is to be sustained:

So far, the extraordinary sales growth laid out in Anthropic’s S-1 has put wind in the company’s sails, providing something more tangible than the promise of AI’s transformative potential. While profitability is by no means assured, it’s possible to see a credible path toward it.

Even if the IPO falls short of the initial reported valuation of $2 trillion , Bloomberg Opinion colleague Chris Hughes suggests that figure should be within reach by 2029. But that forecast comes with a caveat, Bloomberg Intelligence’s Mandeep Singh points out. The company has almost $450 billion in compute commitments through 2030. That means it has to sustain its premium pricing compared to competitors such as Google’s Gemini, OpenAI’s ChatGPT and xAI’s Grok:

Intensifying price competition could make it more difficult to preserve gross margin while generating enough enterprise token consumption to absorb these infrastructure commitments.

The big hyperscalers — Alphabet, Meta Platforms, Amazon.com and Microsoft — all now have wide economic moats to bolster their pricing. It’s not possible for all the frontier AI labs to preserve them in perpetuity, and it is possible that one or more will fall by the wayside. Last year’s DeepSeek moment showed how vulnerable they remain to disruption.

More realistically, they can hope to maintain sufficient competitive edge to sustain healthy margins, possibly through high barriers to entry. Paradoxically, Thomas Tzitzouris of Strategas’ fixed-income team argues that regulations to slow down data-center construction, which look likely since the midterms, could help in this by making it harder for smaller competitors to build capacity and raise capital. If new facilities might never be completed, he argues, it “strangles smaller players’ access to” financial markets:

Why would I want to lend against that? I think this strengthens the argument for big tech players to slow down, improve their cash flow metrics in the intermediate term, and continue to reap the benefits of almost-oligopoly pricing.

Anthropic’s IPO will be a yardstick for Sam Altman’s OpenAI, expected to go public next year. The sheer size of the offerings could test the market’s capacity. If OpenAI does go public, Nathan Sonnenberg of Pitcairn argues, investors can commit only so much capital before having to sell something else to make room:

And markets tend to die one of two ways: if the Fed raises rates too much... or if you get too much supply in the market. We’ve also seen a lot of challenges to lesser IPOs. The market doesn’t want lower-quality names, as there’s a lot of concern about the longevity, the inevitability, and the profitability of AI.

Bob Elliott of Unlimited Funds argues that how this IPO trades will be crucial. Without strong performance, “investors would likely increasingly question whether all the planned capex can pay for itself as expected.” The extraordinary profit growth of the last few years has been dependent on high and accelerating capex, so this is crucial.

With all of that to consider, and the forthcoming publication of Nvidia Corp. results amid the ongoing spy-thriller-cum-soap-opera that is Jensen Huang’s affair with a possible Chinese spy, this looks like a pivotal month for the AI trade.

Survival Tips

When in Rome, do as the Romans. Therefore, when in South America, go to a football match. I’ve taken advantage of a reporting trip in the region to see Sao Paulo draw with deadly local rivals Corinthians in Santos, and then Boca Juniors clinch the Argentine championship with a 95th-minute goal. Several agencies (I used this one ) can get club season-ticket holders to chaperone you through the whole process, which was a great deal.

Neither game was particularly good; I saw a total of one goal. But that didn’t really matter. It’s fun, and you learn a lot. Pele played his entire career for Santos in a stadium that holds 16,000. It was intense, deafening, and seriously uncomfortable — there were places where you couldn’t even stand up:

English fans claim that flair players like Pele couldn’t survive a “rainy night in Stoke”; it turns out that he spent his entire career in just such conditions.

Meanwhile, at Boca’s Bombonera stadium, home of Diego Maradona, many fans were waving Swedish flags:

Why? Boca got their blue with a yellow stripe uniforms in 1906 because of a clash with another club that wore black and white. They decided to adopt the flag of the first ship to enter the port of Boca the next day — and it was Swedish. They now have a Swedish shirts sponsor, and can’t get enough of their Swedish flags. If you’re ever in this part of the world, give it a try.
