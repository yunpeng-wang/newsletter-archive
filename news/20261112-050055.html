The Honey-Trap Discount

The revelations keep coming, and they make it ever clearer that Nvidia Corp. had badly underestimated the key-man risk it was taking in the person of its hugely powerful and respected CEO Jensen Huang. It’s possible, however, that the market is overstating quite how much peril is rolled up in him.

A bigger and more difficult question is whether Huang was also a key man for the entire artificial intelligence industry . So far, the bet seems to be that he isn’t. This is what’s happened to shares in Nvidia and the main exchange-traded fund tracking the Nasdaq 100 since the start of trading last Friday:

After the weekend’s extraordinary revelation of a relationship with an alleged Chinese spy (who has now reappeared in Beijing ), the news that he also had an affair with Kimberly Powell , who heads Nvidia’s efforts to introduce AI to healthcare and reports directly to him, might well have been the final straw. It’s hard to see how any executive could survive after transgressions like this.

At the time of writing, Nvidia’s board was meeting and appeared highly likely to dismiss him .

Huang had been a hugely successful leader. However, with no succession plan in place, all this is bad news and reason to sell off. But even before the revelations of the second affair, traders had taken down Nvidia’s valuation to the point where its forward earnings multiple, on a blended 12-month basis, was only 66% that of the S&P 500 as a whole. Three years ago, it was more than 200% more expensive than the market:

The slide predates the scandal, but still seems remarkable. Nvidia’s prodigious earnings, combined with mediocre stock market performance by the other members of tech’s Magnificent Seven, mean that it hasn’t lost that much ground. Nvidia surrendered its position as the largest company by market cap to Apple Inc. last month; it’s still bigger than any of the other Magnificents:

What’s perhaps most surprising is how bland all of their performances have been. There’s much talk of a bubble, and fear that the market has grown overconcentrated, but none of the Magnificents have looked like either an inflating or a bursting bubble over the last 12 hectic months. Broadly, they’ve gone sideways.

And despite all the heavy trading and swings, the overall index remains extremely narrow. Concentration has reduced since the S&P 500 topped five weeks ago, but not by that much. On Oct. 6, the top 10 stocks accounted for 40.8% of the S&P 500. That’s now down to 38.7% — still historically high. (Note that both numbers might slightly understate the issue because the S&P 500 does not yet include SpaceX, which would be among the top 10.)

So it “feels” like a bubble is bursting, and stocks have reshuffled manically all year, but the overall result looks nothing like that. The reason may lie in the nature of investment in AI. Speculative bubbles are inevitable during the adoption of any radical new technology where gains are far in the future and uncertain. From railroads to the internet, past great innovations have transformed the economy after losing some people’s shirts along the way.

What’s different about AI is what Viktor Shvets of Macquarie calls a “rolling bubble”:

There is no way to hedge the “AI trade,” as it is everywhere. Instead, embracing AI is a better way forward: diversify across a wide range of themes shaped by AI... identify new “bubbles” while discarding past winners that are likely to derate rapidly; broaden exposures outside indices, both listed and private. Our world is not one of a single AI bubble but multiple rolling bubbles.

New research from the index provider MSCI shows how this process has worked out. They divided the AI phenomenon into a series of sectors that would benefit in different ways. Most recently, physical infrastructure (chips and data-center providers, shown in turquoise on the chart) formed what looks like a classic bubble that now appears to be bursting. Previously, the lead belonged to digital infrastructure:

The veteran investor Jim Paulsen suggests that there has been a series of “Busted Bulls.” It’s a strange phenomenon with few analogues, and it’s still unclear whether this is fundamentally negative:

Is a bull market which chronically suffers from “Busted Bulls” one which is constantly refreshing itself allowing the bull market to continue as new bullish themes emerge? Or, as each bull theme cracks, is the overall bull market weakened and made more vulnerable increasing the possibility that a bear market does eventually conquer? Maybe the answer is neither?

If this is truly a rolling bubble, then maybe the instant judgment that the Huang scandal will do terrible damage to Nvidia without hurting the market could make sense. But we can expect it to be tested — and if this story doesn’t force everyone to take corporate governance more seriously when they invest, then nothing will.

Silver Anniversary for China and the WTO

China’s admission to the World Trade Organization traces back to 1972, when then-US President Richard Nixon flew to Beijing to meet Mao Zedong, setting in motion a historic rapprochement . The gradual normalization was punctuated by disputes over trade practices and human rights abuses. The key moment came almost three decades later on Nov. 10, 2001, when the WTO’s trade ministers approved China’s accession at a Doha conference . Beijing became the organization’s 143rd member a month later.

The significance in the aftermath of the 9/11 terrorist attacks was missed at the time, and it’s now overshadowed by President Donald Trump’s sweeping efforts to reshape global trade and erode the WTO’s influence. But even though China’s trade surplus with the US has been under pressure since Trump began a tariff war between the countries in 2018, its surplus with the rest of the world continues to reach new highs:

China’s stock market, however, is a different story. Notoriously volatile, it has had three separate bubbles in the last quarter-century, in 2007, 2015 and 2021. All burst, largely because of crude government interventions — but only in the last year did it fall behind emerging markets and the world as a whole:

What is going on? The trajectory of China’s trade surplus is unsettling for many on the receiving end. Opposition to Chinese imports is growing, but it’s unclear whether this can derail the export growth on which Beijing now relies as it attempts to fix the imploded property market. Last month, exports surged by more than 35% year-on-year, underpinned by technology shipments. The trend is upward:

Concerns over sustaining this growth are harder to ignore as producer inflation accelerated to its highest since 2022. Prices at the factory gate climbed 5.7% in October from a year earlier after a gain of 5% in September, according to the National Bureau of Statistics:

Surging producer prices contrast with subdued consumer inflation, which slipped more than forecast to 0.7% in October. Macquarie’s Shvets attributes this divergence to persistent and growing overcapacity, which in turn results in disinflationary pressures and lower returns. This limits the possibilities to drive growth domestically:

Outside of computers, capacity utilization rates are falling across the board. This is especially true of the automotive sector (66% vs 81% in 2018), but is also visible across most industrial pursuits (e.g. chemicals, coal, electronic equipment). Inevitably, this leads to high unemployment, especially among younger cohorts.

The energy shock for Chinese manufacturers is driving costs higher elsewhere. Unless business owners can absorb some of the rising costs, higher inflation will weigh on global demand for Chinese imports. It’s hard to gauge how meaningful this will be, but October’s exports, which fell below estimates, could signal that it’s already happening.

Beyond price-induced pressures, Macquarie’s Larry Hu and Yuxiao Zhang argue that export growth will slow down. The extent will be shaped largely by the tech cycle, which will in turn determine how much domestic stimulus Beijing needs to provide to meet the GDP target. Whatever new mix results might just revive Chinese equities, which have now given up almost all of their gains since the underwhelming policy pivot in late 2024:

China’s balancing act is difficult, but not impossible. WTO accession unlocked an era of export-led growth, but that has provoked a reaction and stock markets are running out of patience. The challenge now is to find a new source of momentum at home, before limits on the old model are imposed from abroad.
