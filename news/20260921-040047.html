AI Red Flag

Worries about the safety of new technology didn’t start with artificial intelligence. More than 160 years ago, the United Kingdom faced a dilemma when steam-powered cars broke into a transport system dominated by horses. In 1865, the government responded with the Locomotive Act, known as the Red Flag Act , which required a minimum crew of three for each vehicle. One had to walk at least 60 yards ahead, carrying a red flag to warn pedestrians and horse-drawn traffic, assist frightened horses, and signal the driver to stop.

Does AI really need something similar, and will calls to slow the technology down look similar in hindsight? If warnings of catastrophe are right, humanity may not survive to find out; or perhaps more likely the current anxiety may be remembered as a new Red Flag Act. Before then, the pressing question for investors is whether AI can be made safer and still deliver returns commensurate with the massive investment that has poured into the trade.

As noted in last month’s first installment of AIndicators , which track measures of credit-market confidence in AI as well as spending, any signs of trouble will emerge first in the credit markets — and so far, spreads have yet to signal any great trouble. Now the question is whether that can hold through a hiking cycle.

The debt-financed AI data-center buildout is already confronting a market repricing, with higher long-dated Treasury yields raising the cost of capital. This is taking place as the Federal Reserve tightens policy in response to an energy shock rather than an economic boom that might help grow revenue.

Massive AI-driven corporate borrowing was already pushing up long-term yields as companies competed more aggressively for investor capital; a point Fed Chair Kevin Warsh made last week in comments that appeared to prepare the way for a tightening cycle .

Barclays’ duration-times-spread (DTS) – which combines credit spreads and interest-rate duration to measure a bond portfolio’s overall exposure to the risk of declining confidence in ability to repay – shows how much influence Big Tech now wields over the credit market . As of Sept. 14, six groups (Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, Oracle, SpaceX and Microsoft) accounted for 8.4% of the overall spread risk in the US investment-grade corporate bond market. That’s more than the six largest banks, which have historically mattered far more to credit markets.

How might this unravel if the AI buildout goes south? Pimco’s Lotfi Karoui argues that for hyperscaler creditors, the first-order effect is disappointing returns on their investments in AI — not great for their shareholders. Paradoxically, the second-order effect could be credit-positive: capex falls, free cash flow recovers, and the issuance pipeline shrinks:

The same shock that undermines the AI investment case could improve hyperscaler credit metrics. The catch is speed: Competitive dynamics, sunk power costs, lease commitments, and contracted equipment purchases could keep capex sticky even in a downturn, so the paradox only holds if capital discipline follows quickly.

For equity investors, that would hit the hyperscalers particularly hard. Their valuations remain elevated relative to their five-year historical level, even if the margins they generate offer a fundamental justification for those lofty prices, which is an important distinction from the dot-com bubble :

Markets seem to be working on the assumption that optimistic earnings forecasts for hyperscalers are accurate, but aren’t prepared to offer them any valuation premium. UBS’s hyperscalers index is trading at much the same multiple of blended future earnings as the S&P 500 — although they are still at a premium to the equal-weighted version of the index:

Lingering valuation concerns are tempered by the technology giants’ earnings power. Their high margins are among the clearest signs that this market is not built on froth alone — and should also reassure creditors:

Unless frontier AI developers can break free from the prisoner’s dilemma and agree to a slowdown, hyperscalers are likely to keep piling money into the picks and shovels needed to sustain it. That should limit their cash flows, while helping the providers of their semiconductors and memory chips. The main indexes covering the sector took a major correction in the early summer, but have now stabilized:

Market prices still suggest investors have faith in growing demand for the underlying technology. Data from OpenRouter, which tracks the consumption of tokens — the basic units of text processed by large language models — show a dip in usage in recent weeks, without altering the broader direction of travel:

Further, a recent slight uptick in the LLM Token Expenditure Index, which measures effective spending on large language models by combining token prices and usage, offers some reassurance. But it’s still far below its highs from June: As token volumes surge and prices fall, users are increasingly pushed toward cheaper models:

There’s no evidence that calls to tap the brakes on frontier models are damaging the AI trade. That’s partly because Washington is unwilling to tolerate “SICK conspiracy” concerns. But with JPMorgan expecting more than $4.1 trillion of the $5.5 trillion in AI capex to be debt-financed, Gavekal Research’s Will Denyer suggests that rising cost of capital will act where politics has not. He says it poses the biggest threat to the AI infrastructure boom, with Fed tightening adding pressure:

Real yields have been rising amid heavy capital-raising by AI companies and government borrowing to fund growing entitlements and costly wars. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is trying to counter these forces with buybacks at the long end of the Treasury curve, but so far his purchases have disappointed in scale.

Denyer sees rising long-term yields making life harder for heavy borrowers, even if investors’ appetite for their debt seems yield-agnostic. To gauge what might deter such AI investment, he invokes the “Wicksellian spread.” Whenever the spread between the US corporate sector’s real return on invested capital and the real cost of capital falls below its 20-year median, the probability of a generalized investment slowdown rises sharply:

The spread hasn’t yet crossed this threshold, suggesting short rates and bond yields can move somewhat higher before seriously dampening capex. That’s good news for equities, but not for bonds. Denyer adds:

The indicator is imprecise, and the spread is getting uncomfortably close to the threshold. Any further rise in real financing costs will therefore increase the risk of slowing the AI boom in a way that no blog post from Dario Amodei [Anthropic’s CEO] can.

Regardless of how this plays out, the AI investment cycle is due for a reckoning. But it’ll be a while before we know whether today’s concerns are prescient, or our own red-flag moment.

Turning Japanese

Earlier this month, Points of Return proclaimed that “it seems a stronger yen is for real.” It’s always dangerous to make such assertions — much like Charlie Brown expecting that Lucy will finally let him kick the football — and so it has proved again. The Bank of Japan hiked as expected last week, but declined to back that move with the kind of hawkish commentary we had heard earlier from Kevin Warsh at the Fed. Playing the role of Lucy, minus the blue dress, is the central bank’s governor, Kazuo Ueda.

The yen had started this month looking oversold, with many speculators betting against it, and was primed to move. News that Norway’s huge Norges Fund was proposing reallocating its fixed income portfolio in a way that implied selling dollars and buying yen then provided the catalyst for a strengthening. Between them, the Fed and BOJ have now undone much of that move:

It might seem that markets are asking a lot. The BOJ’s target rate is its highest since 1995, and the pace of tightening the fastest since 1990. The future course of rates embedded in overnight index swaps didn’t move after the meeting. But the BOJ voted only 7-2 to tighten (with both dissenters appointed by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi). And Ueda talked down back-to-back hikes, which he said (according to Societe Generale’s translation) were “typically reserved for situations where inflation is extremely high and exceeding the target.” It’s currently below 2%.

It still makes sense to expect hikes ahead. Ueda said financial conditions remain accommodative, and implied further hikes will depend on wage negotiations. Real wage growth has sprung into life; outside of rebounds following major slowdowns, salaries are growing their fastest in decades. But the BOJ wants to see this continue before taking any actions that might choke it off:

In the short run, SocGen’s Kit Juckes suggests the yen could be challenged following a big switch in speculative positioning over the summer. Investors are now long the yen, and that will make it vulnerable, particularly as the dollar rally picks up pace:

Last Tuesday, CFTC positioning showed a net long JPY position that was nearly as large as when USD/JPY fell to 140 in 2025... There are surely more long positions that can still be flushed out, particularly among skeptics of the notion that the yen will rally “just because Scott Bessent wants it to.”

In the longer run, the yen should continue its gradual appreciation. But the path will not be smooth. That makes life tougher for Japanese officials. For the rest of the world. a sudden stop to cheap funding via the yen carry trade (borrowing in yen to park elsewhere) looks less of a risk than it did a few days ago.

Survival Tips

It’s Yom Kippur, the holiest day in the Jewish calendar, when the world’s Jews get together to atone for their sins of the previous year. It’s not on the face of it an appealing prospect, involving fasting for 25 hours and spending most of the time in a synagogue — but plenty of people who are otherwise secular observe it and find comfort in the ancient rituals.
