Long Bonds’ Long March

So what’s new? As I return from a late-summer break, long Treasury yields are still rising, and the stock market is still tolerating them. It’s an international phenomenon; French and German yields are at 18- and 15-year highs, while international stocks, as measured by FTSE, are still hovering around records. This seems alarming, but the chances are that this state of affairs will persist, with variations, for a long time.

Let’s start with some context. The 10-year Treasury yield is below the 5% landmark it briefly set three years ago, but it’s consolidating at the highest levels since the onset of the Global Financial Crisis. While higher than it’s been in almost 20 years, it’s also lower than for almost all of the four decades that preceded 2007:

This is less a crisis than a return to normality after the weird conditions, and often desperate interventions, of the post-GFC era. Some cosy assumptions must now be jettisoned. But there is no reason why a capitalist economy cannot prosper with 10-year yields of 5%. Indeed, a higher yield shows investors believe the economy is growing and may need higher rates in future.

That said, there remains the risk of a discontinuous shock. An orderly rise in yields (like this one to date) gives everyone time to adjust. It may well crimp profits, but it needn’t drive a crisis. Sudden, sharp rises, however, can cause a cascading meltdown. So why exactly are yields rising? And what is the risk of a true disorderly bond accident?

The Fed and the Data

Naturally, the central bank is a key player. The Federal Open Market Committee meets next week, and it’s regarded as close to a toss-up whether it hikes. An unconvincing performance from the chairman, Kevin Warsh, at the July meeting left many suspecting that the Fed would hold again — but he shifted perception with surprisingly hawkish talk at the Jackson Hole symposium. Then last week’s US job numbers for August, well above forecasts, buoyed expectations of a hike once more:

The employment market shows no signs of overheating. One measure of this comes from the Atlanta Fed’s Wagetracker , based on census data, which tracks wage rises achieved by people switching jobs, compared to those who stay. Generally, switchers do better — otherwise they wouldn’t be moving. Brief periods when they do worse tend to come in recessions. Strong returns, as in the aftermath of the pandemic, show upward pressures on salaries. After a slump last year, the jobs market appears to be in equilibrium:

So everything comes down to inflation, with numbers due on Friday. If it rises, a hike would seem nailed down. But there are more dovish noises. Fed Governor Chris Waller put a positive gloss on the current trend last week :

Three-month core inflation is 3.05% for the three months through July, a level that is still not consistent with the FOMC’s 2% goal. Nevertheless, it is important to note the trend. Three-month inflation has fallen steadily from 4.76% in February. That is a considerable improvement, and the speed of this downward trajectory is encouraging.

If the Federal Reserve is able to look at August’s numbers through similarly rose-tinted spectacles, they might still be able to avoid a hike.

Buyers and Sellers

Rising bond yields aren’t solely about rising short-term target yields. Investors will generally demand a premium on top of this to compensate for other risks — known in the lingo as the term premium . Many factors go into this, but the issue is synthesized nicely by Marion Le Morhedec of Fidelity International:

At what yield is there sufficient private sector demand to absorb the growing supply of long duration debt? If that required yield is rising, investors are demanding greater compensation not just for expected policy rates, but for inflation uncertainty, fiscal risk and supply. That matters because policy expectations can reverse relatively quickly when growth slows, whereas a structurally higher term premium can prove more persistent.

If investors are particularly worried about a growing supply of bonds to finance a rising deficit, for example, they will demand a higher term premium and yields will go up. That plainly happened between 2021 and 2024, but for the past year, the term premium as measured by Bloomberg Economics has been fairly stable while yields have edged up:

Torsten Slok of Apollo Management noted that the US term premium is lower than those of Japan and Germany. That pushes back on the notion that the US deficit, or the Fed’s credibility, is forcing yields upward:

Put differently, the Fed went into 2026 expecting several cuts, and now the FOMC is leaning toward hiking. With this backdrop, it is not surprising that long rates are higher.

Stocks

What of the impact bonds could have on the stock market? So far, the growth in earnings, propelled by lenient taxation and the money pouring into the artificial intelligence buildout, has more than counteracted rising bond yields — but the bond market shift has had an effect on valuations. Forward earnings multiples, on a blended 12-month basis, have come down sharply to 19.4 from their peak of 23 last October.

That is largely because of the bond market. When yields are rising, and offering a more attractive return, stocks generally need to become cheaper (a phenomenon known as the Fed Model after Alan Greenspan appeared to apply it). When bond yields rise, then theoretically stocks’ earnings yields (the inverse of the price/earnings ratio) must also rise. That’s what’s happened:

Stocks were a compellingly good deal while bond yields were being held artificially low. Now, with a normalizing Treasury market, valuation will become more contentious. But so far the equity market is adapting. If there’s something to fear, it’s not a continued upward trend in bond yields, but that the earnings everyone is banking on might not materialize.

Emerging Sentiments

Very strangely, high Treasury yields are coinciding with a surge in an index tracking emerging-market currencies, which climbed to a record high ahead of next week’s meeting. It’s doing this even as a Bloomberg Economics gauge of Fed officials’ sentiment signals growing hawkishness — which in normal circumstances will pull money out of emerging markets:

That’s in line with a broader phenomenon. Even as the odds of an increase in the fed funds rate next week increase, the dollar isn’t benefiting as it normally would. Whatever is moving the US currency, it doesn’t appear to be rate differentials:

Even before we get Friday’s CPI data, then, the trajectory of EM currencies suggests that the traditional playbook is breaking down. The latest display of resilience isn’t so much about dollar weakness as a series of favorable idiosyncratic factors that are shoring up the developing world. Even as the war in the Middle East drags on, fears of an energy-driven inflation shock have largely been averted. Brent crude futures remain about 20% below their April peaks, even as the absence of a sustainable peace deal suggests prices will remain uncomfortably high:

EM central banks’ monetary policy is once again proactive, just as during the post-pandemic inflation surge. High real interest rates and stronger fiscal positions in parts of the developing world offer investors both income and a place to ride out the volatility shaking the biggest bond markets. As JPMorgan’s Pierre-Yves Bareau puts it, this “makes EMs more attractive as they act as an income diversifier.”

Local-currency emerging-market bonds returned about 3.6% this year, compared with a loss of about 0.6% for US Treasuries and European peers, according to Bloomberg. This has helped the MSCI EM currency index to rise for 11 straight weeks, its longest streak since 2007.

Rising commodity prices also help. Since July, the Bloomberg Commodity Spot Index has surged by over 15%, strengthening the fiscal and external positions of commodity exporters even as it punishes oil importers.

In Latin America, the combination of the AI buildout and a supply squeeze has driven copper prices higher, bolstering the investment case for miners. This partly explains equity ETFs’ record $3.6 billion surge in global net inflows in the first seven months of 2026, almost double the $1.8 billion recorded over all of 2025, according to Morningstar.

Madeleine Black of Morningstar points out:

Two forces have shaped Latin American equities this year: the Middle East conflict’s pressure on oil prices and the AI infrastructure boom’s demand for copper. That has lifted Petrobras and Grupo Mexico, which together accounted for 40% of the region’s return year-to-date through August 2026.

Copper set a fresh record Monday, suggesting these inflows should persist:

As Points of Return noted here , emerging markets also owe their resilience to semiconductor exports from Taiwan and South Korea. Once their big chipmakers are excluded, BCA Research’s Arthur Budaghyan shows that EM stocks have neither broken out of their trading range nor outperformed developed market peers. AI’s greatest beneficiaries, for now, may be in the emerging world. And that helps to explain how EM assets are holding firm even as rate differentials move against them.
