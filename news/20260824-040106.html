Elbows Out

Tariff wars are back. Last week, US-Canada talks broke down , and Washington levied 50% tariffs on a range of goods. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, elected last year on a platform to resist Donald Trump’s declared aim to make the country the 51st state, responded with a speech announcing truly reciprocal tariffs . There will be one-for-one retaliation with 50% levies.

It was already close to politically impossible for Carney to back down. His speech was as uncompromising as it gets and dug him in even further:

Last spring, I warned that America is trying to break us so they can own us. And I promise that that will never ever happen. We are keeping that promise.

How much does this matter to global markets? Last year, the rollout of US tariffs prompted a global market freakout that calmed swiftly after authorities announced a three-month delay. Levies not-much-less stringent than those unveiled in April 2025 are now in force, and haven’t stopped global markets from setting new records.

One key reason for this, though, was the response. Following the well-established game theory on dealing with bullies , the expectation was for the rest of the world to retaliate. They would inflict pain on themselves but even more on the bully. In the event, only China retaliated in a big way, and eventually forced the US to desist — armed with crucial leverage from its control over rare-earth minerals.

Canada doesn’t have quite such leverage, but Carney believes he has some. He commented:

Canada fuels American growth, supplying 99% of their natural gas imports, 85% of their electricity imports, 60% of their crude oil imports. I don’t think they want us to stop sending any of that energy.

Canada is also, overall, a bigger trading partner to the US than China. Overtaken some two decades ago as the “China shock” of cheap manufactured goods played out, in the last year it regained the lead:

Canadian exports have been impressively resilient. But getting ahead again also owes to the Chinese strategy of diversifying its exports to other destinations, arousing claims of dumping in the process. China fought last year’s tariff hostilities to a standstill, but it wants to reduce dependence on the US in future, and that’s what it’s doing.

This is harder for Canada to do, but Carney has been trying. He claimed that it was now “the best connected economy in the world” after signing “more than 20 trade and security deals across five continents.”

The world is raising its economic drawbridges. That’s profoundly negative, and continues the long descent from an order of cooperation and open markets. In the short term, we’ll see who can take the most pain. There’s no reason to expect a repetition of the Liberation Day swoon, but for investors it will still hurt.

Active Versus Passive, Again

The logic behind the rise of passive investing is known well enough. The mass of active investors, in their totality, sum to the same return as the overall index; passive funds, forgoing the costs of trying to beat the index, undercut them on price; and over time, that cost advantage means index funds will win.

Last week brought the latest installment of S&P Global’s regular SPIVA report , covering 2025, to ram that home. Over 10 years, 87% of US equity funds had lagged their index after fees. Fixed income funds, where costs are more of a difference-maker, have also underperformed:

The last time a majority of active funds beat the S&P was in 2009, when the Global Financial Crisis had created bargains that roared back to life. Active funds lagged in all but three years since 2000:

Over history, even the greatest active fund managers have found it hard to keep compounding gains. Generally, they have particular strategies that don’t work indefinitely, and they come up against capacity constraints; strong performance attracts money, and it’s harder to outperform with a large fund where any one good stock pick will make less difference. This is what happened to Bill Miller’s vehicle, the Legg Mason (later ClearBridge) Value Trust after its 15-year streak of beating the S&P, and to Peter Lynch’s Fidelity Magellan fund:

Compounding outperformance can generate astounding wealth but it’s very hard to maintain. The latest SPIVA report makes that clear yet again.

Two other points. First, last year should have been good for active managers because it saw the highest dispersion (variation between the best and worst stocks) since 2009. As happened then, such dispersion should be just what’s needed to allow stockpickers to outperform. Why didn’t they? The problems seems to have been the massive outperformance of the biggest five stocks, whose dominance was greatest when dispersion was highest early in the year:

To beat the index, active managers had to pile in to those stocks, leaving their portfolios unbalanced. That was a big ask — and there’s an argument (covered elsewhere by Bloomberg Opinion colleague Aaron Brown ) that the sheer weight of passive managers is making those mega caps ever bigger, and outperformance all the harder.

A second issue, a little late now that passive management has won the intellectual debate and the contest for investors’ dollars, is whether SPIVA overstates active managers’ disadvantages. A group of academics led by Martijn Cremers of the University of Notre Dame published a paper examining the 2024 report and found three problems:

  • It considers any fund that exits the sample to have underperformed.
  • It weights all funds equally, even though most assets are invested in a small number of large funds.
  • It compares funds against hypothetical, zero-cost benchmarks, rather than against investable, low-cost options (such as ETFs).

Making these adjustments changes the story a bit. The proportion of US equity funds lagging over five years dropped to 56.2% from 73.7%, while over 20 years the drop was to 55% from 99.2%. The case for passive remains, but it’s not as overwhelming as it appears — or at least before the weight of money in index funds made active managers’ lives even harder.

One safe prediction: This issue will not go away.

Making Crypto Great Again

Bitcoin’s uninspiring run this year is making a turn for the better. It needed a catalyst and Wednesday’s announcement from the Treasury Department that it will double the size of buybacks for longer-dated securities jolted the debasement trade — a bet against fiat currencies like the dollar – back into life. The cryptocurrency surged as much as 23% in the days following Secretary Scott Bessent’s decision:

The lifeline drove the biggest daily gains since February. The asset class is known for its wild swings, but the recent foray into an unusual uneventful patch made these gains even more pronounced:

Trading activity has expanded after months of depressed volumes. Coinbase recorded transactions worth at least $1.72 billion between Wednesday and Thursday, compared to a 30-day average of $367.5 million. The surge only underscores how unusually low the bar had become.

More than 10 months after Bitcoin began a plunge of nearly 50% from its peak, it still needs an even stronger catalyst to reclaim former glory. If last week’s strong inflows into Bitcoin exchange-traded funds are anything to go by, investors are starting to believe again. The harder question is how durable that conviction will be:

Bitcoin’s rally continued even after bonds swiftly reversed their gains. What matters now is just how hard Bessent tries to put a lid on Treasury yields . If he continues on this course, the dollar, which fell as much as 1% after the announcement, remains another key variable. Further tumbles, together with a cap on yields, would likely keep money flowing into Bitcoin ETFs. However, as Bitunix’s Dean Chen argues:

Conversely, if the $40 trillion debt burden and persistent inflation pressures push the term premium higher again, forcing the Federal Reserve toward tighter policy, the current high-beta rally across crypto assets could face another round of repricing pressure.

Stephane Ouellette of Frnt Financial argues that the bullish narratives that drove Bitcoin to record highs last year haven’t gone away, and crypto’s infratructure continues to develop.

But there remains a sense that the regulatory policy framework needs to be clearer. The Senate’s failure to take up the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act before this month’s recess pushed the next major possible catalyst to September. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Nathan Dean expects the Commodity Futures Trading Commission to push for broader access to crypto and greater regulatory certainty, which might lower the barriers to institutional adoption.

Given Bitcoin’s spectacular collapse since October, there’s no guarantee that such policies would make a difference, but the combination of favorable policy (apparently a Trump administration priority) and a macroeconomic pivot might be just what it needs.

Crypto’s safe haven reputation has been badly damaged. It has recently declined during a period of rising inflation - the precise environment where the haven/inflation-hedge thesis predicts it should outperform. And the Iran conflict still presents meaningful risks.

Crypto enthusiasts will bask in Bessent’s bounty for the time being. But the uneventful months before his move should not be forgotten. Bitcoin will need to sustain the next leg of its recovery long after this burst of enthusiasm dies down.
