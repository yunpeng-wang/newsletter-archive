We’ve Been Here Before...

These political waters are not uncharted, and these markets are certainly not unprecedented. For a parallel to guide through to the conclusion of 2026, the best guide might be 2018.

At first glimpse that’s bad news. That midterm election year ended with a savage selloff, culminating with a brutal curtailed session on Dec. 24 instantly dubbed the Christmas Eve Massacre . But the good news is that it was swiftly reversed, and gave way to a really good market rally in 2019 (before Covid-19 the following year ate everything up). If 2018 is the guide, we should brace for some turbulence, and a good buying opportunity:

Naturally, this all plays out against a backdrop of significantly higher inflation, rates and bond yields, while 2018 saw no oil shock to match what has happened this year. But the parallels are close.

Then, as now, President Donald Trump was in the White House, growing increasingly unpopular and facing the possibility of a Democratic “Blue Wave” taking control of Congress. In 2018, as now, markets were surfing on the results of a massive Trump cut in corporate taxes that fed into impressive profit growth.

And just as in 2018, the Federal Reserve is under new management. Kevin Warsh, like Jerome Powell before him, has started by laying out his intention to shrink the central bank’s massive balance sheet, swollen by years of buying up bonds. He’s been greeted by a sharp rise in the fed funds rate expected for the end of the year, as measured by the Bloomberg World Interest Rate Probability function:

Back then, Treasury yields were beginning to rise after a decade of QE-induced anesthesia. Powell wanted to help this process further by selling off the Fed’s huge bond portfolio “on auto-pilot.” Digging through the archives, this is what Points of Return said on Nov. 5, 2018 , on the eve of the midterms:

The story of 2018 for investors continues to be the bond market’s attempt to adapt to the new realities for the economy and monetary policy, and the attempt by other markets to adapt to the new reality of the bond market, which is settling into a new, higher trading range in yields.

That sounds exactly like today. Harry Colvin of Longview Economics said:

There are so many parallels with 2018, aren’t there? You had a resilient earnings-led equity market, where forward earnings on the S&P were growing at about 20% year-on-year, and early in the year you had consumer confidence towards the stock market at all-time highs. We’re back at those kind of levels. Retail money is all in and fund managers’ most crowded trade is long equities .

He added that global breadth was declining with markets dominated by a few stocks. Then the elections created uncertainty and resulted in a clear Democratic victory, but was nevertheless short of the kind of crushing sweep that polls suggest could be possible next month.

What would it take to keep the replay going? Colvin suggests that we should indeed brace for something very similar, with months of 2018-like serious turbulence followed by a buying opportunity:

For the next three to six months, it’s a sideways trend and a bounce in volatility. Until we reset rates at the front end.

That scenario would require the Fed to cooperate. At this point in 2018, the market started betting that Powell wouldn’t be as hawkish as billed. With the Fed’s language staying hawkish, it then took the brutal Christmas selloff to provoke the notorious “Powell Pivot.” The result was a total upending of expectations. At this point in 2018, markets were discounting a rise to 3% for the fed funds rate by the end of the following year. In the event, rates would be cut to 1.5%:

For this scenario to play out, expectations for the Fed would need to start declining soon, possibly in reaction to stock market turbulence that the midterms result could exacerbate. At present, the market is still braced for three more hikes by the middle of next year.

Risks lie in the two key points of difference. The first is earnings growth, fueled by big corporate tax cuts. While growth was impressive in 2018, expectations have surged even more this year, largely thanks to the spectacular profits being realized by a few of the biggest beneficiaries of AI. This is how Bloomberg’s consensus estimates moved from 2017-18, and again from the beginning of 2025 to date:

Any substantial disappointment to those earnings hopes would be magnified by valuations. Eight years ago, the huge intervention that followed the Global Financial Crisis was only starting to unwind, and 10-year Treasury yields were far lower than the earnings yield (the inverse of the price/earnings ratio) on stocks. Stocks steadily derated through the year before the drastic selloff at the end. This year is nothing like that — stocks have derated a little, but not as much, while bond yields have surged. Over these last eight years, the safety net has been removed from under the stock market:

There’s one other added risk that could arise from the elections, by changing expectations for 2028. Pictet’s Luca Paolini warns that the populist left might get taken far more seriously, and with it the chances of tax rises and heavy new AI regulation after 2028, if their highest-profile candidates win.

He also suggests watching California’s Proposition 40 to tax billionaires — which has prompted Meta’s CEO Mark Zuckerberg to consider leaving the state . “If that passes, the music completely changes,” Paolini said. Higher taxes are not currently taken seriously as a risk by markets. If that were to change, it could endanger the likelihood of a 2019-style buying opportunity.

AI Bubbles

The notion of an artificial intelligence bubble keeps winning converts, with Citadel’s Ken Griffin now joining Bridgewater founder Ray Dalio . That bubble is not bursting yet, thanks to continuing robust earnings, most recently from Meta Platforms. But CoreWeave’s recent troubles suggest the hype is not totally indiscriminate, and that there are real doubts.

Share prices aren’t retreating, but there’s clear evidence that investors are hedging against the risk that massive AI investment disappoints by taking out insurance against default. That shows up in the rise in the implicit default risk signaled by the credit default swaps of some hyperscalers:

It also shows up in a massive increase in activity. Trading volume for CDS tied to the 10 largest hyperscalers and chipmakers surged 691% to a record $140 billion this year through September. That’s up from around $17.7 billion in the same period last year, although some swaps started trading only recently. Oddly, there is no sign of this stress in the spreads at which hyperscalers’ traditional bonds are trading.

The pickup in activity dates back to early in the year when hyperscalers began reporting negative free cash flow. It doesn’t signal that the sky is falling, but rising credit default swap prices generally point to higher default risk, which is never good. As Societe Generale illustrates, widening CDS spreads are coinciding with deteriorating 12-month forward P/E multiples. Hyperscalers’ valuations now sit near levels not seen since ChatGPT’s launch four years ago:

SocGen’s Manish Kabra argues that wider CDS spreads are translating into lower multiples because markets are discounting higher funding costs and longer payback periods. Until free cash flow improves, credit conditions might matter more for valuations than earnings revisions:

The signal to buy hyperscalers is not stronger earnings per share, but a peak in CDS spreads... Until credit markets stabilize, we remain underweight hyperscalers. We would become more constructive closer to the second half of 2027, when free-cash-flow inflection begins to outweigh funding concerns and the derating phase is likely behind us.

Such concerns could put a lid on massive AI borrowing. However, Bloomberg Intelligence analysis suggests that strong growth in the backlog for almost all the hyperscale cloud providers will drive them into spending even more on capex. BI’s proprietary data-center model shows an aggregate backlog of around $2.5 trillion, which is driving the updated capital-outlay assumption of $1.36 trillion in 2026, an almost 100% increase compared to 2025. For next year, BI forecasts total capex of $2 trillion, including SpaceX as an additional contributor to spending:

The question is whether wider CDS spreads reflect worries about supply or about credit quality. It’s possible investors want more compensation to absorb record issuance, and that they’re not protecting against a higher default risk. Hyperscalers’ balance sheets started out at robust levels and remain strong.
