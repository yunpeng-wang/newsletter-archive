Correction 3 of Trump 2.0

Politics aren’t supposed to matter much to markets, but in the age of Trump they plainly do. In two years, Donald Trump has overseen an impressive bull market in global stocks, driven in large part by his policies. He’s also overseen three corrections, all forced largely by presidential mistakes:

The Republicans’ dreadful midterm election results sparked a global negative reaction. But it was highly selective. Five of the 11 main sectors of the S&P 500 made gains last week. Only two — technology and energy — suffered severe losses:

Many uncertainties cloud the next two years, while the election of 2028 now looks harder to call. But the Federal Reserve continues as before, and there’s little chance for fiscal policy to shift in either direction. Democrats cannot enforce business-unfriendly regulation over the Trump veto. And companies have unveiled yet more great results for the third quarter , even if a few disappointed rosy expectations. The Nasdaq 100 now trades at a multiple of blended 12-month forward projected earnings that it hasn’t seen since Covid’s worst days:

Unless the political risks are truly profound, it looks like a potential buying opportunity. Those risks center on two perceived effects of the midterms: They will make it harder to build AI data centers , and may also hasten a possible end to the Iran war.

Data Centers

Voters faced 41 local ballot initiatives on data centers, and rejected all but one. To quote George Noble of The Noble Report, “That one number tells you more about the next two years than anything else that came out of the midterms.” Opposition appears organic, and it behooves politicians to heed it.

It also behooves AI companies, who have thus far relied on the support of the Trump White House, to make their case to a skeptical public. It’s not clear they can. The result, according to Louis-Vincent Gave of Gavekal Research:

The phase of hyper-growth is now behind us. First for financial reasons, but secondly, and much more importantly, for political reasons. The power just drained away from Trump. So “building stuff” will now no longer be the priority. Voters (and thus politicians) have clearly shown that they no longer want hundreds of billions of dollars directed into the build-out of data centers.

As the money pouring into the data centers was driving higher bond yields that much of the world could not support, this is not necessarily a bad thing. A sudden stop remains unlikely. Democrats now in power need to be pragmatic. To quote Larry McDonald of Bear Traps Report, Gina Hinojosa, the new Texas governor, has a balancing act:

The hyperscalers, as we speak, are likely threatening to leave the state for Arizona or other locations — possibly even across the border in Mexico. The governor-elect doesn’t want to lose hundreds of thousands of jobs. Most likely, she moves to the center, and that sets up a nice relief rally in semiconductors

That’s not yet clear, but valuations that assumed as a certainty that politicians would egg on the pace of AI development needed to fall, and they have.

Iran

Polls, and common sense, suggest that electors are punishing Trump for attacking Iran. Vice President JD Vance himself laid out that position Wednesday on social media. He cited a poll of Republican voters who voted against the party and were asked what would prompt them to return:

The top four answers, accounting for nearly all of the responses were:
1) End the war
2) Focus on cost of living/inflation
3) Put America First
4) Change our relationship to Israel

All are variations on the same theme — that the US should pull out of the war. Trump’s appointment of Stephen Miller (who has also criticized the war) as his new chief of staff could be a further signal of intent.

As ever, investors tried to preempt military moves. Monday and Wednesday, marked on the chart, saw huge selloffs for crude:

As for Vance’s tweet, it was immediately interpreted as a foreign policy signal. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution responded to it by saying, “The war is now de facto over.” The overall oil market seemed to agree:

There are contrarian arguments. Trump’s behavior in the last week, most obviously in appointing Miller, suggests that he plans to double down. Before the midterms, there were fears he would try to protect his legacy by escalating in Iran. A “lame duck” has nothing to lose, and can be dangerous.

The behavior of the last few days make that look less likely. A deal to end hostilities will be very difficult, so prices for delivery a year from now remain above $80 per barrel. But the assumption that the war would intensify has been reversed dramatically:

The politics of AI and oil remain to be resolved. It’s possible that two big forces driving up inflation and interest rates will be moderated. For now, energy and tech stocks naturally sold off. That explains the latest Trump correction.

Software Power

The AI trade brought a new rule — any reversal in sentiment toward artificial intelligence eased the pressure on software companies. Their business models were expected to sustain critical damage from the growth of AI . Now there are also signs that some of these companies have become more resilient, and are using AI to shore up their businesses rather than letting it undermine them.

The gap between the performance of the Nasdaq 100 and the S&P 1500 software sector over the last 12 months has narrowed almost to nothing:

The so-called “SaaSpocalypse” has yet to hit software-as-a-service companies . Recent earnings calls hint at how they’re dealing with existential threats. From ServiceNow Inc. to IBM Corp. and Adobe Inc., executives claim that AI-driven productivity gains and cost savings have helped them shore up margins. ServiceNow’s Chief Financial Officer Gina Mastantuono said AI deployment had helped realize more than $1 billion in annualized value and lifted operating margin to 32.5%, 150 basis points above guidance.

Other anecdotal evidence suggests that fears have been overblown. One gauge of the attention companies are giving the issue is to track mentions of “artificial intelligence,” “AI,” and related terms during their earnings calls. They appear to have plateaued:

Whether this is really translating into meaningful productivity gains is another matter, but it should be only a matter of time before conclusive evidence emerges. For now, the earnings power of the S&P Composite 1500 Software index suggests resilience:

If anything, the steady rise – regardless of macro conditions or rate volatility — in the index’s forward 12-month consensus EPS offers the strongest evidence yet of software companies’ enduring earnings strength:

AI may yet lower barriers to entry and allow smaller companies into markets traditionally dominated by larger software providers — as BCA Research’s Peter Berezin has suggested. Last year’s DeepSeek moment showed AI-fueled disruption can be swift and even catastrophic.

If software companies’ best option is to use AI to boost margins, that might turn a reversal in the AI trade into a negative for them. It would help to explain why the software cohort has sold off alongside AI in the last week.

Software groups also face a refinancing cliff. About $200 billion in debt is due to mature over the next two years, much of it raised when rates were near zero.

With refinancing costs doubling, there is more need for caution. As TwentyFour Asset Management’s Danny Zaid says: “The market might just look so much different three years from now.” But at least there’s no apocalypse now .

—Richard Abbey

Meanwhile, in the Macroeconomy

Last week’s download of unemployment data seemed oddly unimportant given the political drama. But it did spring a surprise. Americans don’t like their economy. Yet the Bureau of Labor Statistics showed, against expectation, that the unemployment rate was falling. For those of prime working age, unemployment is at levels usually only seen during economic booms:

One implication is that recession looks very unlikely. In September 2024 (marked in the chart below), a swift rise in the jobless rate prodded the Federal Reserve into a “jumbo” rate cut of 50 basis points. The Sahm Rule , named for Bloomberg Opinion colleague Claudia Sahm , found that when the unemployment rate rose by 0.5 percentage points in six months, a recession resulted.

That threshold was breached in August 2024. It has turned out to be a false alarm. A recession is nowhere near, and neither is any justification for rate cuts:

With rate cuts off the agenda, expectations for the Fed stabilized. At the end of this September, with bond yields rising fast, investors priced fed funds to rise beyond 4.75% next year to ensure the central bank’s credibility. They had given up on one hike before the election; last week’s stock market selloff, which makes it harder to hike, brought it down to about 4.25%:
