FOM Wait and C...

Saint Augustine of Hippo had a prayer for the Federal Reserve. “Lord give me chastity and continence,” he prayed, “but not yet.” He was expressing a contradiction in human nature that doesn’t go away. Most of us find it as pressing now as the young saint did 1600 years ago.

And similarly, Kevin Warsh and his colleagues on the Federal Open Market Committee want the tighter monetary policy that will be needed to restore the price stability that they admit remains elusive. But not yet.

It had been widely trailed that the Fed would pause at this meeting before hiking again in December, so this was no surprise. But the language in the agreed statement was, if anything, a tad more positive about the economy than expected (and therefore more supportive of a rate rise). In September, when the Fed raised rates, economic activity was increasing at a “solid” pace. Now, it’s growing at a “strong” pace — so the Fed thinks the economy is accelerating further, despite that hike.

Two members of the FOMC dissented in favor of a hike, surprisingly including the governor Lisa Cook, who is being pursued by the Trump administration over allegations of irregularities in a mortgage application. So it’s obvious that a hike at this meeting was well and truly aired. Why not just get on with the job and hike in October? After all, the Bank of Canada had bitten the bullet a few hours earlier.

Once Warsh’s press conference — which you can see here — reached its Q&A section, Colby Smith of the New York Times started by asking exactly that: “Why not just raise rates today?”

Warsh said that he’d expected the question. His response lasted three minutes and 16 seconds. He never came close to answering it. Among the lines he offered during his ramble:

  • “We’ve doubled down on the 2% target.” [It’s been in place informally since 1996 and formally since 2012.]
  • “We’ve laid out our framework in Jackson Hole .” [That’s what central bankers do when they’re in Wyoming.]
  • “We didn’t just talk about acting. In September we acted.” [We knew that — the question was why they didn’t act again in October.]
  • “The period right in front of us is information-rich.” [More so than other periods? Or is this code for knowing the midterm results by December?]
  • “We interrogated reality.” [Good, but it would be alarming if the Fed didn’t do that.]

Later, he denied that anyone had ever even mentioned that midterm elections were next week, and in his last response made perhaps his most interesting statement: “The goal isn’t to throw sand in the the gears of the economy and throw people out of work.” Again, it would have been very big news if he had said the opposite. But many took his emphasis and choice of words as a “tell” that this Fed isn’t prepared to risk forcing a recession. Florian Ielpo of Lombard Odier argued:

The message is nevertheless clear: The Fed is not prepared to tighten policy to the point of engineering a slowdown. Markets could welcome that restraint, provided the forthcoming data support the Fed’s assessment .

The sum effect is to leave rate expectations exactly where they were after the last FOMC. The market still expects fed funds to peak at about 4.6% within the next 12 months. The brief excursion to much higher expectations amid the selloff in Treasury bonds, which always looked overdone, has been emphatically reined in:

Others made a better case for the pause. Byron Anderson, head of fixed income at Laffer Tengler Investments, argued:

Divided government is more than likely, a debt ceiling fight is coming in early ’27, Iran strikes could be possible shortly. Uncertainty and volatility are going to stay elevated, and there is no reason for the Fed to pour gasoline on the fire with rate hikes that will do nothing.

But there was something disconcerting about an evasive press conference that was often delivered in the style of a kindergarten teacher (setting the right policy is about finding the right mix of “monetary medicine,” apparently), and marked by Warsh’s refusal to allow follow-up questions.

This arguably prejudiced another justification for holding, which is that the rise in bond yields since the last meeting has done the Fed’s tightening for it. Ali Hassan of Thornburg Investment Management cautioned that Warsh’s refusal to give forward guidance “risks being self-defeating,” as two-year yields eased during his conference.

Overall market reaction was tame, but a rise in market-based inflation expectations, which had been under control, suggest that Fed credibility was coming into question:

The last two spikes in expectations can be attributed to policy own-goals by the administration; this one looks like the Fed’s fault.

As for the stock market, the juxtaposition between the reaction to Warsh’s words and the results from Alphabet Inc. and Microsoft Corp. tended to confirm that these days, hyperscalers are of macroeconomic importance :

Hypersensitive Hyperscalers

Tech companies have justified their rising capital expenditures as necessary to deliver the much-needed return on the huge investments in artificial intelligence. Productivity can’t be revolutionized without data centers. But it looks as though the hyperscalers are heeding the skeptics — both Alphabet and Microsoft reported capex notably lower than had been predicted.

But as markets grew warier about surging costs, hyperscalers’ revenues offer little reassurance. Oracle Corp. guided at its most recent earnings conference that positive free cash flows would return in 2029, providing credible evidence that markets want a balance between buildout costs and the payoff.

When Alphabet missed analysts’ sales projections, the reaction was brutal. Its sales, excluding partner payouts, were $111.2 billion for the three months that ended Sept. 30, slightly missing analysts’ projections. Its great cash-generating machine, advertising revenue, totaled $82 billion, below estimates of $83.6 billion. Shares fell as much as 10% after-hours:

The disappointment is understandable, given growing concerns about the hundreds of billions of dollars the company has poured into AI infrastructure. Less clear is whether the selloff is an overreaction or can be justified by the underlying risks. Technology analyst Luke Lango suggests the reaction is excessive. He argues that while earnings per share at $2.12 were far below the expected $3.02 expected, much of that “miss” was an $8.66 billion paper loss on Alphabet’s stakes in other companies, largely SpaceX. This, according to him, is “accounting noise, not a broken business.”

More problematic, this was the second successive quarter of negative free cash flow. When the company’s stock sales are taken into account, it appear that shareholders are now helping to fund the AI buildout ( much as is happening at Meta Platforms Inc. ). The company can no longer rely solely on its own Google-driven cash generation. That’s what the market is punishing, Lango argues; this is a “how do we pay for it problem” and not a “nobody wants it” one:

Alphabet’s capex plan is intact, and the spending is about to accelerate into the fourth quarter. If you own the companies selling the picks and shovels, tonight is good news. For Alphabet stock, maybe not.

Another plausible explanation is that investors are focusing on form over substance. As Oracle’s guidance reinforces, they’re growing impatient for tech companies to start reaping returns from AI. This complicates future spending prospects, as Apex Fintech Solutions’ Mike Treacy points out. He suggests it’s only a matter of time before market pressures force companies to moderate capex. The question is whether such a pullback would delay the potential returns, or signal a deeper loss of faith in the technology:

What we’ve seen in the past is tech companies tend to move in herds, and so it will be interesting to see what the domino effect is from Oracle deciding to moderate their spending.

The contrasting response to Microsoft’s earnings showed that traders will still reward companies that can demonstrate their investments are paying off. It reported better-than-expected cloud revenue, with closely watched Azure sales growing 48% to $33.2 billion in the quarter ended in September. Overall revenue rose 19% to $92.3 billion, offering compelling evidence that Microsoft is beginning to translate its AI spending into tangible returns.

Moreover, as Lango points out, Microsoft “did it while spending less than feared: Capex was $45.2 billion against a $45.9 billion estimate. That’s the opposite of what got Meta punished last week .”

The deeper lesson from this earnings season is also growing clearer. Stock markets — and not just debt investors — are uncomfortable with the scale of the current outlays. In AI at least, we have probably passed Peak Capex.

—Richard Abbey

To stuff his conversation full of quibble and of quiddity,
