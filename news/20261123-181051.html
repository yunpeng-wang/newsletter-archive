Oh Jensen!

You know who has a very funny job right now? Joon Kim, the Wachtell Lipton partner and former federal prosecutor who was hired by Nvidia Corp. two weeks ago “to conduct a thorough and independent investigation” “ in light of recent reporting that founder and CEO Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort.” When Kim got the job on Nov. 10, nothing was decided. In theory, Kim’s thorough and independent investigation could find that Huang did nothing at all wrong and should keep his job at Nvidia, or that he had, like, 37 affairs with spies and had to go. Nvidia’s board of directors would receive Kim’s report and use it to decide Huang’s fate.

But then, two days later, Nvidia announced that Huang had resigned, which presumably colored what the board expected from Kim’s report. A report concluding that Huang did nothing especially wrong would make the directors look bad: They had forced out a visionary founder, and erased $1.2 trillion of shareholder value, for no good reason. Can’t have that. After Nov. 12, Kim’s implicit job was to find as many affairs as possible.

Not anymore, though! One assumes that he’s going through the draft today hastily deleting affairs. Bloomberg’s Molly Schuetz reports :

Nvidia Corp.’s board reinstated Jensen Huang as president and chief executive officer, less than two weeks after his remarkable ouster, after US government intervention. Nvidia shares gained more than 31%.

“The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead Nvidia, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers,” said Harvey C. Jones, board chair, in a company statement Monday.

Huang’s return is effective immediately, according to the statement. The board reinstated Huang after a series of meetings and discussions and after receiving input from “senior levels of the United States government,” according to the company. “Based on that input, the board is confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.”

Here is the press release . Obviously the funniest outcome would be:

  • Kim and the independent directors conduct their investigation thoroughly and impartially;
  • They find lots more bad stuff; and
  • The board fires Huang again in a month.

What is the probability of that? It can’t be zero , can it? The Trump administration apparently wanted Nvidia to reinstate Huang, but all of the reporting suggests that the Trump administration itself has pretty divided views about the Huang scandal, and maybe next month it will change its mind too. The board is currently “satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang’s candor and ability to lead NVIDIA,” but presumably it was also satisfied with his candor and leadership a month ago, before it learned about the affairs, which suggests that it could once again become unsatisfied. Kim is a former federal prosecutor who is presumably billing by the hour and who has lots of incentives, not least sheer comedy, to go wherever the facts take him. The directors “are essentially deciding they’re going to roll the dice” on the results of the investigation, Columbia law professor Eric Talley told the New York Times .

A few points here. First, I said on the Money Stuff podcast last week that there is a certain technicality to all of this. There seems to be a view that certain actions — having an affair with an alleged spy, having an affair with a subordinate , having another affair with another subordinate — are disqualifying, in the sense that, if a CEO does them, the board has no choice but to remove him. But these bright-line rules do not give any guidance for what happens next . “If your CEO does X, you have to fire him,” fine, but then what? Then you have to hire a new CEO. Whom do you hire? The most qualified candidate. What if it’s the old CEO? Apparently that’s fine! Misconduct in his previous job (as CEO of Nvidia) is not disqualifying for his next job (as CEO of Nvidia). The board discharged its obligation by getting rid of Huang, so now it is free to bring him back.

Second, the involvement of the US government here is very strange. The board all but says “the government told us to bring him back.” President Donald Trump told CNBC that he talked to the board and wanted Huang back. (I want to read the board minutes of that phone call.) Huang “built a multibillion-dollar business to feed the ravenous appetite of Chinese customers for American chips,” with data centers in Malaysia “packed with top-of-the-line Nvidia chips China’s leading AI companies can use to challenge American dominance,” and “lobbied Washington hard to keep the backdoor to China open”; also he had an affair with an alleged Chinese spy who worked to get Nvidia chips to China . Nvidia’s board apparently concluded, two weeks ago, that all this was bad for US national security and, thus, for business. And then the president called the board up and said “actually it’s fine.” Okay! Do you think that the reinstated Huang will be more or less enthusiastic about selling chips to Chinese companies?

Third, the artificial-intelligence trade kind of collapsed last week: Nvidia was down 22.9%, the Nasdaq 100 Index was down 10.4%, and there were headlines like “ Has the AI Bubble Already Burst? ” Why? I mean, obviously the market took Huang’s ouster (the previous week) poorly, but there was a lot else going on: Anthropic PBC’s initial public offering brought a lot of supply to the market, and you could point to various fundamental business reasons that people might have been worried about the AI trade. Nvidia’s stock peaked in early October, and trouble has been brewing for a while. If you had asked me on Friday “why are AI stocks down this week,” I would have mentioned Huang’s ouster, but I wouldn’t have stopped there.

And then Nvidia brought back Huang and, by noon today, the Nasdaq was up about 7% and Nvidia was up about 32%. It’s not that all of the AI trade is just about Jensen Huang’s personal leadership, but, like, half of it is. Seems like a lot. There is something … charming? reassuring? stupid? … about the power of the Great Man Theory of the AI Trade. [1] “All of these matrix multiplications are creating a new silicon God more powerful than you can possibly imagine, one that will usher humanity into an age of miracles or possibly destroy us entirely, but it all depends on this one guy’s love life.” There’s this sort of inverse Omelas , where our post-human utopia/dystopia depends on one man having affairs.

Last, and related, what does this say about formal governance structures? As I mentioned last week , Anthropic has a lovingly crafted, extremely complex system of two boards and five share classes to make sure that, if it ever has to fire its CEO, it will be for the right reasons. Elon Musk has said that he needs to own at least 25% of Tesla’s stock to make sure that he can’t be fired as CEO unless he goes insane. Mark Zuckerberg periodically tries to reshuffle Meta Platforms Inc.’s share classes to entrench his control. There is a popular view that visionary tech founder-CEOs need to have significant voting power, in the form of economic ownership or supervoting shares or other governance rights, to make sure that they do not lose control of their companies. And outside investors seem happy to agree to this, because they want the visionary CEOs to pursue their passions, and are suspicious of professional independent directors who might not understand the vision.

But then Sam Altman was forced out of OpenAI , where he owned no stock, and he came back three days later more powerful than before. Jensen Huang was forced out of Nvidia, where he owns about 3% of the stock and has no supervoting rights, and he’s back two weeks later. He had no formal control of Nvidia, but he had a lot of support from employees and business partners and Elon Musk and Donald Trump, so he was able to overrule the board anyway. It turns out that billionaire visionary AI CEOs have a lot of informal power, and a lot of people who are eager to help them out.

Chips in space

Everyone is borrowing billions of dollars to build data centers for artificial-intelligence training and inference. At a high level, the lenders’ credit analysis has two main components:

  1. Most of the data centers have long-term leases with big hyperscalers or frontier AI labs, and the expected lease payments will cover the payments on the debt. [2] So as long as the relevant tenant — OpenAI or Anthropic or Meta or SpaceX or whoever — keeps paying for compute, the lenders will get paid back.
  2. Even if the tenant stops paying — because it goes bankrupt, or gets out of the AI business, or whatever — someone else will still want to use the data center. The data centers are filled with advanced AI chips, and the overall demand for AI chips will be robust even if one particular company gets into trouble. The AI boom will have winners and losers, and this one tenant might be a loser, but the chips can quickly be redirected to the winners.

Obviously the general downside case for the credit is something like: “Actually, if the tenant stops paying, there will probably be some correlated drop in demand for these AI chips — because the AI boom goes bust, or because this generation of chips becomes obsolete faster than expected, or because someone finds a way to do training and inference with much less compute, etc. — and you won’t actually be able to re-rent your chips to anyone else.” [3] The main risk, for the lenders, is that the chips they are financing turn out to be less valuable than they think.

But there are other, more idiosyncratic risks. The data center might never get leased, because it never gets built, because local governments don’t give it the necessary permits or for some other “force majeure” reason .

Or another one might be: The tenant stops making lease payments, and the lenders go to repossess the chips to re-rent them to some other big AI lab, and they open the doors to the data center and the chips aren’t there. Just tumbleweed blowing through the data center. This is not that likely. The data centers are big and full of lots of server racks, and the racks require lots of specialized equipment, and it is not easy to stuff them in a sack and fly them to China, and presumably someone is monitoring them.

Unless, of course, the data center is already in a spaceship . The Financial Times reported Thursday :

Lenders backing SpaceX’s latest $43bn debt package, which is secured by its advanced AI chips, have tried to bar Elon Musk from firing the collateral into space as part of his plan for orbital data centres.

The world’s richest man has outlined ambitious plans to power AI models with data centres cooled by the vacuum of space. That will require packing satellites full of advanced chips.

SpaceX has said it plans to use the chips involved in the debt deal on Earth. But lenders have expressed concerns that if the group later decided to shoot the valuable chips that underpin their loans into space, it would prevent them from reclaiming and selling the semiconductors in the event of a default, according to people familiar with the matter.

It is uncertain whether the provision was included in the finalised documents.

The bonds were issued by a special purpose vehicle designed to keep the debt off SpaceX’s balance sheet and preserve its investment-grade credit rating.

Never have I wanted to see a movie more than I want to see the Elon Musk Space Repo Movie. Never has a movie written itself more than this movie writes itself. There’s some scruffy Han Solo/ Lone Starr -type space repo guy flying out to SpaceX’s space data centers to load its servers into his space Winnebago, and then Elon Musk comes flying in in a TIE fighter to blast him, and there’s a space dogfight where both sides are extremely careful not to damage the valuable collateral. There’s a scene back on Earth where the lenders show up at Jay Newman’s slightly defective Miami condo and say “Jay, we know you are retired, but you are the last person to have seized a warship over a credit dispute , do you think you could do that with a spaceship,” and he gets a dark faraway look in his eye and puts on a spacesuit and says “let’s roll.” Also of course there are scenes in conference rooms where the lawyers argue over what constitutes an event of default and whether flying the chips into the wrong part of space allows the lenders to seek recourse from SpaceX’s balance sheet. And then they show up at SpaceX’s launch site to seek recourse from its balance sheet and an even bigger laser battle breaks out. Absolutely all of my interests in one place.

Two things. First, we talked about this financing in October, when the Financial Times reported :

Investors whom SpaceX has previously approached about financing its multibillion-dollar chip purchase said they only received a short two-page deal memo with pictures of outer space and an arrow pointing out that the company was going to build data centres “somewhere in the universe”.

You can see why they might have asked for a covenant! I wrote :

I feel like “we are going to buy billions of dollars of chips directly from Nvidia, and then we are going to put them somewhere” is actually a pretty coherent thesis that can, and probably will, support billions of dollars of debt. I guess if you take a lien on the chips as collateral, and then SpaceX blasts them into an undisclosed location “somewhere in the universe,” you will have a hard time repossessing them, but SpaceX has an investment-grade rating so it’s probably fine.

But I guess that was too glib, and you want the collateral.

Second, I wrote in 2023 about one of my favorite secured financing stories ever. YieldStreet Inc. got in trouble with the US Securities and Exchange Commission for losing money on some loans to finance ship “deconstruction.” Some companies would buy ships for scrap, sail them to a scrapyard, break them down and sell the scrap for, hopefully, more than they paid for the ships. YieldStreet would lend the companies money to do that, and “the collateral for the loan was the ship to be deconstructed.” But … but … but … but? When it’s time to repay the loan, there is no ship . If the borrower defaults on the loan, there is no collateral to seize, because the whole point of the transaction was to destroy the collateral.

I suppose that is not exactly analogous to the SpaceX situation. SpaceX doesn’t want to destroy these chips. It wants to use them. But if it sends them “somewhere in the universe” and then stops making payments on its bonds, the lenders have a whole universe to search to find their collateral.

Oh Jensen (2)

We have talked a lot around here about a central problem in artificial intelligence, which is that the going rate for good AI employees is enough money to retire immediately. If you want to hire good researchers, you have to pay them $100 million each, and once they have $100 million why should they come to work? We have discussed a number of practical solutions, including (1) the employees’ intrinsic dedication to AI research, (2) the illicit thrill they get from working on products that they think might kill everyone on Earth, (3) San Francisco real estate prices , (4) the notion that “ sudden wealth syndrome ” is bad and can be prevented by work, (5) stock lockups and (6) recursive self-improvement, where the AI gets good enough to do its own AI research and the wealthy AI researchers are no longer required.

Those are some of the ideas I’ve written about, but I am just an outside observer, and it stands to reason that the CEOs of the biggest AI companies would have more and better ideas for how to motivate their centimillionaire workforce. For instance, the New York Times reports on Jensen Huang’s solution :

As Nvidia became wealthier and wealthier in recent years, Jensen Huang grew increasingly frustrated with his staff.

He told groups of subordinates that they had become so rich that he couldn’t motivate them simply with money, according to three people who spoke on the condition of anonymity to discuss confidential conversations. The only way he could grind more work out of them, he added, was through humiliation.

Like a lot of the solutions, this one does not, strictly speaking, make sense. If you have $100 million in the bank from working at your job, and your boss spends all day humiliating you, you could just quit! Then (1) you’d have plenty of money to live a life of abundance and leisure and (2) your boss wouldn’t be around to humiliate you. I say all the time that the most important paper in economics is “ the one about how people sometimes give themselves painful electric shocks just because that is an option that’s available to them.” It explains, among many other phenomena, Nvidia’s human resources philosophy.

This also rhymes a little bit with a great story about Peter Brown of Renaissance Technologies, from Greg Zuckerman’s book The Man Who Solved the Market:

Once, as he worked on a complicated project late in the evening, full of manic energy despite the hour, Brown picked up the phone to call a junior associate at home with a pressing question. A colleague stopped Brown before he could dial.

“Peter, you can’t call him,” he said. “It’s two a.m. ”

Brown looked confused, forcing the colleague to explain himself.

“He doesn’t get paid enough to answer questions at two a.m.”

“Fine, let’s give him a raise, then,” Brown replied.

At Nvidia, everyone gets paid enough to be humiliated.

Backward Hat Capital

If you were coming up with a name for a fake hedge fund, you could do a lot worse than “Backward Hat Capital Management LLC.” The name conjures “finance,” yes, but in a silly way. Just spot-on parody. Anyway, here’s a US Securities and Exchange Commission enforcement action against some guys who named their allegedly fake hedge fund Backward Hat Capital and “fraudulently raised approximately $560 million from more than 2,000 investors,” “made more than $250 million in Ponzi-like payments to previous investors,” and “transferred more than $220 million to themselves to fund their luxurious lifestyles.”

I feel like on balance “Backward Hat Capital” is a more reassuring — less Ponzi-connoting — name for a hedge fund than, you know, Extremely Safe Capital Management LLC, or Connecticut Landscape Feature Capital or whatever. It’s a shame it was allegedly a Ponzi.
